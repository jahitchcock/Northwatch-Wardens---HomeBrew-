# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Chapter One — The Whispered Name

---

I have kept many songs. Some I gathered, some I was given, and some I tore out of silence with my own hands because no one else would say them aloud. This is the first of them, and the last I will ever be able to set down, because it is also my own.

I will begin where the keeping began: with a girl in a dark grove, doing a forbidden thing, and certain she was right.

She was wrong about almost everything that night. I have had a long time to be gentle with her.

---

The grove of remembrance lay at the heart of the Nullwood, and at the heart of the grove stood the memory-trees, and that night I went among them the way I was permitted to go: in silence.

This is how it was done. You walked the rows with your hands open at your sides. You stopped where grief stopped you. You laid your palm to the bark of the one you had come for, and you held in your mind the face you had come to hold, and you said nothing. Not the name. Never the name. The Quiet asked only that one thing of us, and asked it of everyone, and called it mercy.

I laid my hand on my brother's tree. The bark was cool and a little wet, the way it always was after the evening mist came down through the canopy, and under my palm I felt the slow patient life of the wood, which does not hurry and does not forget and does not, whatever we told ourselves, give anything back. I held his face in the dark behind my eyes. I held the particular way he had of laughing before the joke was finished, so that you laughed at his laughing and never learned the joke at all. I held all of it in silence, the way I had been taught, the way we all were taught, and I felt the great soft weight of the Quiet settle over me like snow over a thing left out too long.

And then I did what I had come to do, which was not that.

I leaned close to the bark, close enough that the word would go no further than the wood, and I gave the tree his name. Quietly. The old way. The forbidden way.

*Fenn.*

The grove did not change. No light, no warmth, no sign. The bark took the name the way it takes rain, and gave nothing back, and I stood there with my heart going hard against my ribs and my eyes stinging, fierce and glad and frightened all at once, and I thought: *there. He is still here. I have kept him.*

I was young. I still believed a thing kept was a thing saved.

It would be a long time before I understood what I had truly done in that grove, and longer still before I could forgive myself the arrogance of it — for it was arrogance, though it wore the face of love, the way the cruelest things we do so often wear the face of love. But that is the end of the song, and I have promised to begin at the beginning.

So. Begin again. A girl, a tree, a name spoken where no name was allowed.

And, three rows over, a light.

---

I saw it as a paleness first, a thinning of the dark between the trunks, and my whole body went still before my mind had named the thing. A lantern. Shuttered down to a slit, the way the Thornwatch carried them on the night rounds, so that the light fell only on the path and never woke the sleeping wood.

A sentry, walking the rows.

I did not run. Running is sound, and sound is the one thing the Quiet listens for. I lowered myself instead, slow as the mist, until I was kneeling in the wet leaf-mould at the foot of my brother's tree, and I made myself part of its shadow, and I breathed against my own sleeve so the breath would not show in the cold.

The light came on. I heard the soft creak of harnessed leather, the press of a boot finding the silent places between roots — even the Thornwatch walked the grove quietly; we were a people who had made quiet into a kind of faith. The lantern-slit swung left, and right, and lingered. I watched the pale bar of it slide up the trunk beside mine and stop, a hand's breadth above my bowed head, close enough that I could see the grain of the bark lit gold and every other thing drowned in black.

I did not breathe. I thought, with a child's certainty even then, that the tree would keep me the way I had asked it to keep my brother. That the wood was on my side. That the silence I was breaking would, just this once, break gently for me.

The light held. Held. A slow age of it, my knees soaking through, my pulse so loud in my own ears I was sure it carried.

Then the lantern-slit swung away, and the boots moved on, and the paleness thinned back into the dark between the trunks and was gone, and I knelt there in the cold and the wet and let the breath out of me in one long shaking thread.

I had done this many nights. That is the part I find hardest to tell, now, with the years between us — not that I broke the Quiet once, in grief, which any heart might forgive, but that I had made a habit of it. I had come and come and come again, and each time the not-being-caught had felt like the wood's blessing, like proof, and each time I had stayed a little longer and risked a little more, and never once asked myself what I would do when the night came that the lantern did not swing away.

That night was coming. I could not have known how soon. But I think, kneeling there with my heart still going and the leaf-mould cold against my shins, some small honest part of me already did — the part that is always a little wiser than we let it be — because I remember what I did before I rose and slipped back toward the sleeping village.

I laid my hand once more against the bark, in the dark, where no light could find it.

And I did not say his name again. Not because I had learned anything.

Only because I was afraid.

---

The way home wound down from the grove through the lower terraces, where the great trees grew close enough that our people had long ago learned to live between them rather than beneath. We did not cut the Nullwood. We asked it for room, and over the long patience of elven years it gave us room — a hollow here, a wide branch there, a place where two trunks leaned together and made a wall. The Verdant Mother had taught us, the old songs said, that you do not take a home from the world; you let the world grow you one. By the time I was born, the whole village was held in the wood that way, walkways woven between the boles, doors that were really the spaces between roots, our lamps dimmed to the dull green of foxfire so the canopy would not lose its dark.

It is a beautiful thing to remember, and I have made it beautiful in the telling, and I will not pretend that is the whole of it.

Because we had done the same thing to our grief that we had done to the wood. We had not cut it down. We had built around it, quietly, year by year, until the silence was not a thing we kept but a thing we lived inside — a house with no windows that we had all helped to raise and none of us could any longer find the door of. That was the Quiet. Not a law, by the time I came to it. An architecture. We had grown ourselves a home for the unspoken, and then we had moved into it, every one of us, and learned to call the dark good.

I passed a window where old Aewen sat awake, as she always sat awake, a candle behind her and her face turned to it, holding whatever she held. She did not look at me and I did not look at her. We let each other keep our silences. That was the only courtesy we had left to give.

My own door was the gap between two roots at the foot of a hemlock older than any name we had for it. I went in quiet. I had grown very good at quiet; we all had; it was the one art the Quiet truly taught.

The house slept. My mother's door was closed, and behind it I heard nothing, which told me nothing — she had become as practiced at silence as the rest of us, and could lie awake inside it for whole nights without a sound, and often did. I did not go to her. There is a particular cowardice that looks exactly like consideration, and I had grown good at that, too.

I stopped, instead, the way I always stopped, at the place by the cold hearth where my brother's cloak still hung.

No one had moved it. That is the thing about a silence so complete — it cannot speak even to say *take this down now, it has been long enough.* So the cloak stayed, year upon year, on its peg by the hearth, and we walked past it, and we did not look at it, and we did not say what it was, and in this way we kept Fenn in the house the only way the Quiet allowed: as a shape we had all agreed not to see.

I looked at it. I made myself look at it, the way I had made myself look at his tree. And I did not say his name, because I had said it once already that night and used up all the courage in me, and there was none left for the harder thing, which would have been to say it here, in the house, where it might have reached the one person it would have wounded.

I went up to my own small dark and I lay in it with my eyes open.

I was not a wise girl. I want to be honest about that, across all this distance, because the songs we tell of ourselves are forever making us wiser than we were, and it is a kind of lie. I did not lie awake that night understanding that a whole people can love a thing to death by refusing to let it go. I did not see the shape of my own life in the shape of that cloak on its peg. I was a girl who had broken a rule and not been caught, and who was frightened, and who could not have told you whether the fear was of the Thornwatch or of something nearer and harder to name.

But I lay awake. And in the dark I found I was doing the thing I would spend the rest of my long life doing, the thing they had tried so hard to teach all of us not to do.

I was saying his name. Not aloud. I had learned that much fear. But over and over, behind my teeth, in the one place they could not reach — *Fenn, Fenn, Fenn* — keeping him the only way I knew, which was the wrong way, the closed-fist way, the way that holds a thing so tightly the holding becomes its own kind of grave.

It would take me years, and a road I had not yet imagined, and a man who could not die, to learn the difference between keeping a thing and clinging to it.

That is the song I have come to sing you. It begins, as all the old songs do, in the morning — with the bell for the keeper-school, and the first lie I would tell that day, and not the last.

But that is the morning. This was still the night.

I lay in the dark of the house we had built around our silence, and I said my brother's name where no one could hear it, and I did not yet know that I was already, in the only way that ever mattered, refusing to be quiet.
