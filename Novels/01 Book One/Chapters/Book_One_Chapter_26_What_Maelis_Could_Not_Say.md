# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Chapter Twenty-Six — What Maelis Could Not Say

---

Maelis had his own Quiet.

Not the Nullwood's Quiet — not the law, not the architecture of silence that my people had built around their grief. His was smaller and more personal. It was the quiet of a man who had spent his life in the spaces between words, who had learned to hunt by listening, to track by reading, to love by watching from a distance and saying nothing.

He had loved Wren for the better part of a season and never told her. The love was carried the way he carried his bow — close, familiar, a thing that was part of him and yet never quite spoken aloud.

Now she was dead and the words he had not said had nowhere to go.

---

I found him at the edge of the camp, sharpening his knife. The blade did not need sharpening. He had already sharpened it twice that morning. But his hands needed the work, and the work gave his hands something to do while his mind circled the thing it could not reach.

I sat down across from him.

He did not look up. The stone rasped against the blade. Back and forth. Back and forth. The rhythm was steady. The hands were not.

"Maelis."

"Yes."

"What."

He stopped sharpening. His gaze moved from the knife to the ground to the trees beyond the camp, where the birch stand thinned into scrubland and the scrubland rolled south toward the Flats.

"I do not know."

"That is all right."

"I have words. I have always had words. I am a hunter — I name things. I name tracks, I name wind-directions, I name the distance between one thing and another. But this—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "This does not have a name."

---

I waited.

The stone rasped again. Back and forth. The rhythm was a prayer, or a ward, or simply the motion of a man trying to keep his hands busy while his heart broke.

"When I spoke at the grave," I said. "What did you feel?"

He thought about it. "Lighter."

"Yes."

"But also worse. Like something was being pulled out of me that I had been holding for a long time and I did not know if I could bear the pulling."

"Yes."

"Is that what it always feels like?"

"I do not know. It was my first time."

He looked at me then. Really looked. The way he looked at a track when he was reading it — with the whole of his attention, with the patience of a man who knows that the truth of a thing is in its details.

"You were afraid."

"Yes."

"But you did it."

"Yes."

"Why."

I thought about this. The honest answer was not simple.

"Because the alternative was the Quiet. And the Quiet is what killed Fenn a second time. It is what would have killed Wren a second time. I will not let it."

---

He set the knife down.

"Teach me."

The words were small. Three words. But they carried the weight of everything he had not said — the year of silent love, the vigil, the laying-out, the leaving song he had sung with steady hands and an unsteady heart.

"Teach me to do what you did."

"I do not know if it can be taught."

"Try."

I looked at him. The hunter. The laconic man. The one who had stood just outside the love story, watching, guarding, keeping his distance the way he kept distance from all things. And now he was asking me to help him close that distance. To help him say what he had spent a year not saying.

"All right," I said. "Start with her name."

"Wren."

"Again."

"Wren."

"Tell me something true about her. Something small. Not the big things — not the love, not the loss. Something small."

He closed his eyes.

"She tied her boots with a double knot. Every morning. She said a single knot was a lie and a double knot was a promise."

I smiled. "Yes. That's her."

"She hummed when she cooked. Not the old song — just tunes. Little things. She did not know she was doing it."

"Yes."

"She was afraid of deep water. She would not admit it. She said she was cautious, not afraid. But I saw her at the river crossing. Her hands were shaking."

He opened his eyes. They were wet.

"I am not good at this."

"You are."

"I have spent my whole life not speaking."

"Then you have a lot to say."

---

He talked for an hour.

Not in long speeches — Maelis did not have long speeches in him. In fragments. In small true things. In the details of a man who had watched someone closely for a year and noticed everything and said nothing.

He told me about the way Wren walked — slightly ahead of everyone, as though she were always the first to arrive at a place she had not yet reached. He told me about the way she ate — quickly, without ceremony, as though food were fuel and not pleasure. He told me about the night at the Hollow Choir's edge, when she had sat beside him in the dark and said, *"You don't have to say anything. Just sit with me."*

And he had sat. And she had leaned against his shoulder. And he had not moved for fear of breaking whatever spell had brought her there.

He told me all of this. And I kept it. Not in the old way — not hoarded, not hidden, not locked in the hollow of a tree. I kept it in the new way. I spoke it back to him. I said, *"She leaned against you because she trusted you. She trusted you the way she trusted the ground beneath her feet — completely, without thinking, without the need to verify."*

And he heard it. And the hearing was its own kind of keeping.

---

Corwin watched from the edge of the camp.

He had not approached. He had not spoken. He sat on a stone, his hands in his lap, his eyes on the two of us. I could not read his expression. It was not grief — or it was grief, but grief layered with something else. Something that looked, from a distance, like recognition.

He was watching the craft being born. He was watching one mortal teach another how to keep a thing by giving it away. And he was seeing, perhaps for the first time, that the thing he had chased across ages — the keeping, the remembering, the refusal to let the dead vanish — was not a destination. It was a practice. It was something you did, not something you found.

*"I would not understand until much later that Corwin had been trying to keep Wren the wrong way for centuries — holding her close, chasing her across ages, refusing to let her go. He was watching me learn, in a single afternoon, what he had failed to learn in a thousand years."*

---

When Maelis was finished, he picked up his knife again. Not to sharpen it — to sheathe it. The work was done.

"Thank you," he said.

"You did the work."

"You showed me how."

He paused. He looked at the grave, visible through the birch trunks.

"I will carry this back to Thistlebrook."

"The speaking?"

"The practice. The way of it. I will teach it to others. There are people there who have lost and cannot say the names. I will show them."

I nodded. The craft was no longer mine alone. It had passed from me to him, and from him it would pass to others, and from those others it would pass to still more. The giving-away was not just of grief — it was of the method itself. The discipline of speaking the dead aloud, so that nothing true is lost.

Maelis was the first student. He would not be the last.
