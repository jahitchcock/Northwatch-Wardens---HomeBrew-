# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Chapter Ten — The Keeping of Strangers

---

The road taught me what the Nullwood could not. Not in a day, not in a week, but in the slow patient way that roads teach — by showing you the same thing over and over until you see it, by carrying you past the thing you need to understand until the understanding settles into your bones the way dust settles into the cracks of a well-used road.

Corwin did not teach me. I want to be clear about that, because the songs we tell of our teachers are forever making them into something they were not. He was not a teacher. He was a man walking a road, and I was walking beside him, and the walking was the teaching, and the teaching was the thing I had not known I needed until the road gave it to me.

The first thing the road taught me was that I had been wrong about memory.

---

We came to a shrine on the third day south of Greymarch. It was a small thing — a cairn of stones at a crossroads, with a wooden post beside it and a cloth tied to the post, and the cloth was covered in names. Not carved. Written, in ink that had faded and run with the weather, the way ink does when it is left to the mercy of the road and the rain. I stopped to look, because I had never seen a thing like it — a place where strangers had stopped and written the names of their dead and left them there, in the open, for anyone to read.

"What," I said.

Corwin looked at the shrine the way he looked at most things — quickly, efficiently, the way a man looks at a thing he has seen a hundred times and does not need to see again. "A shrine," he said. "People leave names."

"Why."

"Because the dead deserve to be spoken. Even by strangers. Especially by strangers." He paused. "It is an old custom. Older than the road. The road was built to pass the shrine, not the other way around."

I looked at the names. There were dozens of them, maybe more, written in different hands, in different inks, some faded to almost nothing, some still dark and clear. I read one — a woman's name, written in a careful hand, the letters small and precise, the way you write when you are writing something that matters and you want it to last. I did not know the woman. I did not know the person who had written her name. And yet the name was there, on the cloth, in the open, for anyone to read, and the reading was a kind of keeping, and the keeping was a kind of speaking, and the speaking was the thing I had been exiled for doing in secret.

I stood at the shrine and I understood, for the first time, that the Nullwood was not the only place in the world that kept its dead. The Nullwood was the only place in the world that had made keeping into a crime.

---

Corwin watched me at the shrine. I felt him watching, the way you feel a thing in the body before the mind has named it, and I did not turn to look at him because I was not ready to be seen, not yet, not while the understanding was still settling into me the way dust settles into the cracks of a well-used road.

"You did not know," he said. It was not a question.

"No."

"That the whole world keeps its dead. Just differently."

"No."

He was quiet for a moment. Then he said, in the dry warm voice, the voice that held the warmth at arm's length and offered it and withdrew it in the same breath, "The Quiet is the aberration. Not the keeping. The keeping is the thing the world has always done. The Quiet is the thing your people invented because the keeping hurt too much."

I looked at him. He was looking at the road, not at me, and his face was doing the thing it did — the closing, the shutting, the door pulled shut behind the eyes — and I understood, in that moment, that he was not speaking as a man who had read about the Quiet in a book or heard about it from a traveler. He was speaking as a man who had seen the Quiet from the outside, who had come to the stones and asked for the old songs and been turned away, and who had understood, in the turning-away, that the Nullwood had done something to itself that the rest of the world had not.

"How do you know that," I said.

"Because I have been everywhere else. And everywhere else, they speak their dead. They write their names on cloths and tie them to posts. They carve them into stones and set them at the crossroads. They sing them in the evenings, around the fire, the way your people used to sing them before the Quiet. The keeping is the thing the world has always done. The Quiet is the thing your people did when the keeping became too heavy to bear."

I looked at the shrine. I looked at the names. I looked at the cloth, faded and weathered, the names written in different hands, in different inks, the names of strangers kept by strangers, the keeping of a world that had not made silence into a virtue, and I understood, for the first time, that the Nullwood was not the keeper of memory. The Nullwood was the aberration. The Nullwood was the place that had taken the oldest human instinct — the instinct to speak the dead, to keep them alive in the mouth, to lend them a voice so they could go on a little longer in the only world that was left to them — and had turned it inside out, had made it into a crime, had called it mercy, and had believed, for two generations, that the silence was kinder than the speaking.

I had been exiled for doing what the whole world did.

---

We walked on. The road went south, and the country opened, and the sky was enormous, and I walked beside Corwin and I did not speak for a long time, because the understanding was still settling into me, and the settling was a thing that could not be rushed, the way dust cannot be rushed into the cracks of a well-used road.

It was near evening when I asked him the question I had been carrying since the stones, since the boundary, since the morning he had ridden out of the mist and asked the Thornwatch whether anyone within still kept the old songs.

"Why," I said. "Why. What."

He did not answer at once. He walked on, his eyes on the horizon, the way he always watched the horizon, with the particular attention of a man who has learned that the horizon is where trouble comes from and has made a habit of watching it the way other men make a habit of prayer. And then he said, in the dry warm voice, the voice that was the whole of him and the whole of the distance he kept, "I am looking for fragments. Pieces of the world from before it broke."

"Before it broke."

"Before the Sundering. Before the world was made into what it is now. There were songs then — old songs, true songs, the kind that hold a thing against forgetting. I am looking for them. I have been looking for a long time."

"Where."

"Everywhere. Every place that remembers. Every people who still speak their dead. Every shrine, every cairn, every cloth tied to a post with names written on it. I go where the keeping is, and I listen, and I gather what I can, and I move on."

"Why."

He was quiet for a long time. The road went on. The sky darkened. And then he said, "Because there is a place — a ruin, old, older than anything the road passes now — called the Hollow Choir. They say it holds the oldest song of all. The first song. The song that was sung before the world broke, when the world was still whole." He paused. "I am going there. I have been going there for a long time. I have never reached it. But I am going."

I looked at him. He was looking at the horizon, and his face was doing the thing it did — the closing, the shutting, the door pulled shut behind the eyes — and I understood, in that moment, that the quest was not a thing he had chosen. The quest was a thing he was bound to, the way a man is bound to a road he has walked so many times that the walking has become a part of him, and the not-walking would be a kind of death, and the death would be worse than the walking, and the walking would go on, and the going-on would be the thing he did until the thing he was looking for was found, or until the looking was all that was left.

"I am coming," I said.

He looked at me. The door behind his eyes was open — the narrow space, the width of a man who has been offered company and has decided, against his better judgment, to accept it, because the being-alone has become so much a part of him that the not-being-alone feels like a thing he will break if he holds it.

"You are already coming," he said.

---

We made camp that night in a hollow between two hills, where the wind was less and the grass was tall and the stars came out over a world that was larger and louder and more alive than anything the Nullwood had prepared me for. Corwin made a fire, small and efficient, the way he did everything, and I sat beside it and I watched the flames and I thought about the shrine, and the names, and the keeping of strangers, and the Quiet, and the aberration, and the thing I had been exiled for doing that the whole world did, and I felt the understanding settle into me the way dust settles into the cracks of a well-used road, and the settling was good, and the settling was the thing I had not known I needed until the road gave it to me.

Corwin sat across from me, tending the fire, and I watched him and I thought about the old songs, and the Sundering, and the Hollow Choir, and the quest that was not a choice but a binding, and I thought about the man who had asked at the stones whether anyone still kept the old songs, and I thought about the girl who had been standing forty paces away with her dead brother's name behind her teeth, and I thought: *he was looking for me. He was looking for the thing I was doing in secret. He was looking for what my people had made into a crime. He did not know he had found it. The not-knowing was a mercy, and the mercy would walk between us for the rest of my life.*

I did not say any of that. I said, "You said the keeping is the thing the world has always done."

"I did."

"And the Quiet is the aberration."

"It is."

"Then why do you keep. Why do you gather the fragments. Why do you look for the old songs. If the keeping is the thing the world has always done, then the keeping is not special. The keeping is just what people do. Why do you do it as though it were more than that."

He looked at me. The door behind his eyes was open — the narrow space, the width of a man who has been asked a question he has answered too many times and has learned to answer by going still, because the stillness is the only answer that does not cost more than he can pay.

"Because there is a difference," he said, "between keeping a thing and clinging to it."

"What is the difference."

"Keeping is giving a thing a voice. Clinging is refusing to let it go. Keeping is speaking the dead so they can go on a little longer in the only world that is left to them. Clinging is holding them so tightly that the holding becomes its own kind of grave." He paused. "Your people — the Nullwood — they turned keeping into clinging. They held their dead so tightly that the holding became a silence, and the silence became a law they could not undo. The Quiet is what exiled you."

I looked at him. He was looking at the fire. I understood, in that moment, that he was not speaking from a book. He had lived the difference between keeping and clinging. He had not yet learned to stop clinging. The not-learning was his curse, and it would walk with him for the rest of his endless life.

"I don't understand," I said.

"I know," he said. "You will. Eventually. When you have kept enough things, and lost enough things, and learned the difference between keeping and holding, and holding and saving, and saving and loving, and loving and clinging. Clinging kills what you are trying to save."

He looked at the fire. The fire looked back. I did not understand. The not-understanding was a mercy, and the mercy let me sleep.

But that is the rest of the song. This was still the fire, and the stars, and the man, and the girl, and the road south. The not-understanding was the mercy, and the mercy was enough.

We sat by the fire. We were alive. We were together. We were walking south, toward whatever would break us open next. The walking was the thing, and the thing was enough.
