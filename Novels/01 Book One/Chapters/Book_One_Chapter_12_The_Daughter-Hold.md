# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Chapter Twelve — The Daughter-Hold

---

Karrendeep was not Tharundor. The songs tell of the great deep holds of the dwarves, but Karrendeep was not great. It was a daughter-hold, a distant child of Tharundor, and it sat in the hills the way a child sits at the edge of a room full of adults — present, attentive, not yet sure of its place.

Brannoc brought us there on the fifth day after the mine. He brought us the way you bring something home — with care, as though carrying something fragile.

The hold was carved into the hillside, not built but shaped, the way dwarves shape all things — with patience and with song and with the particular care of a people who believe the stone is alive, is listening, is waiting to be asked for room.

We came to the gate. A great arch of stone, carved with runes that caught the light and threw it back in patterns that were not random. A language. A story. The story of the hold, told in stone. Brannoc laid his palm on the runes and read it the way I read a name-slip. He smiled. We were welcome.

---

Inside, the hold was warm. Not hot, not cold, but warm in the way stone is warm when it has been lived in for a long time — when it has held the hands and the voices and the grief of a people who have made it their home.

Brannoc led us through the halls. He showed us the forges, where the dwarves worked the ore they had mined, shaping it with hammer and song. He showed us the ritual-halls, where they gathered before decisions, laying their hands on the stone and listening to the ancestors.

Then he took us to the resonance-archive.

---

The archive was a chamber deep in the hold, carved from the living rock. The walls were smooth and polished and covered in runes — not words but names, not stories but lives, not lists but keepings. The dwarves had carved the names of their dead into the walls. The carving was not a writing but a singing — a singing that the stone held, that the stone remembered, that the stone gave back to anyone who laid a hand on the wall and listened.

Brannoc laid his hand on the wall. He closed his eyes. He listened.

Then he turned to me and said, "Your turn, elf. Lay your hand on the wall. Listen."

I did. I laid my hand on the stone, and the stone was warm. The warmth opened into a sound — not a sound but a vibration, a vibration that was a name, a name that was a life, a dwarf who had lived and worked and loved and died and been carved into the wall and kept by the stone, given back to anyone who listened.

I understood. With my hand on the wall and the stone warm under my palm and the name singing in my ear, I understood that the keeping was not something the Nullwood had invented. The keeping was not something the dwarves had invented. The keeping was something the world had always done — in stone and in voice and in cloth tied to posts. It was universal, necessary, the thing that made the world worth living in.

I took my hand off the wall. "It's the same thing," I said.

"What's the same thing."

"Your keeping and my keeping. You do it with stone. I do it with voice. But it's the same hunger. The same need."

He looked at me. "Aye, elf. It's always been the same thing. Your people just forgot. They turned it inside out. Made it into a crime — the same thing the whole world does, and they made it a crime. And the crime is what exiled you. And the exile brought you here. To see that the keeping is not something you invented. It's a gift. And the gift is what makes you what you are."

---

We stayed in Karrendeep for three days. We ate with the dwarves and slept in the guest-halls. We were warm and fed and, for the first time since the Nullwood, we were not alone. The trio became a family.

On the morning we left, Corwin did a thing that unsettled me.

We were at the gate, saying our goodbyes. The hold-master was there — a dwarf of great age and greater dignity — wishing us well, giving us provisions. Corwin stood beside me, quiet. Then he stepped forward.

"Hold-master. I have a debt to pay."

The hold-master looked at him. "A debt? To Karrendeep? We have no record of a human owing Karrendeep a debt. We keep careful records."

"I know," Corwin said. "The debt is old. Older than your records. Older than your hold. It was owed to the ones who carved this hold, who shaped these halls, who sang these walls into being. It was passed down. It is now owed to you. Paying it lets me walk away clean."

He reached into his pack and took out a small thing — a stone, carved, old, older than anything I had seen him carry. He gave it to the hold-master. The hold-master took it. His face changed.

The hall went quiet.

"This stone is older than Karrendeep," the hold-master said. "Older than the carving of Karrendeep. From the time before the hold was shaped, when the stone was still raw and the shaping was still a dream." He looked at Corwin. "How do you have this. How do you have a thing older than our hold. A debt older than our records. A memory older than our stone."

Corwin looked at him. The door behind his eyes was shut. The wall was back. "I have been around a long time, hold-master. Longer than you know. Longer than your records know. Longer than your stone knows. That is how I have things older than they should be. And that is why I pay the debt and walk away clean."

He turned. He walked out of the hold. He walked onto the road. He walked south.

Brannoc and I stood in the gate and watched him go. The watching made us silent.

Brannoc said, "Your human's a strange one, elf. Stranger than the stone thought. Stranger than the hold thought. I don't understand it. And the not-understanding makes me watch him closer. One day I'll know what he is. And I'm not sure I want to know."

I looked at the road. Corwin walked ahead — his back to us, his face to the south, his eyes on the horizon. My road was behind him now.

We walked on.
