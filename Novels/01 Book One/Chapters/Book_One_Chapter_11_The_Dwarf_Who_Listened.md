# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Chapter Eleven — The Dwarf Who Listened

---

We met Brannoc at a waystation called Stone's Rest, which was not a rest and not particularly stony, but was a low timber building with a yard full of pack-mules and the smell of ore-dust and the particular noise of people who have been working underground and are glad of the sky. The waystation sat at the edge of the dwarven country, where the hills grew steeper and the rock came closer to the surface and the road went from bad to worse and the people grew shorter and broader and louder. The loudness was a kindness I had not expected. The Nullwood had taught me that loudness was to be avoided. The road had taught me that loudness meant you were not alone. And not being alone was what I had walked toward when I walked out of the Nullwood.

Brannoc was at the bar when we came in. A dwarf of middle years, old enough to be suspicious, young enough not to be tired. He was arguing with the waystation keeper about the price of ale, loud and good-natured, the kind of exchange that passes for friendship among people who have known each other long enough.

He saw us come in. He saw Corwin — human, lean, scarred, the look of a man who has walked farther than anyone should — and he saw me — elven, young, the look of a girl who has walked out of a story and is not yet sure what kind — and he called out, in the trade-tongue, with the bluntness dwarves use when addressing strangers: "You two look like you've been walking a long time and not eating enough. Sit down. I'll buy you a bowl of stew and you can tell me what you're running from."

---

We sat. He bought us stew. It was good — thick, with meat and root, the kind of honest food dwarves make. We ate. Brannoc watched us eat. When we were done he said, "Now. What are you running from."

Corwin said, "We are not running from anything."

"Everyone's running from something. Even if it's just the weather." He looked at me. "You. Elf. What did you leave behind."

I looked at him. He was looking at me with the particular attention dwarves give to things they are trying to understand — blunt, direct, not pretending to be gentle. I said, "I left behind a silence that was killing me."

He nodded. He did not ask what I meant. He did not need to. He had left something behind too, and he recognized the same leaving in others. It was why he bought us stew. It was why we sat down together. And it was why, by the time we stood up, we were a trio.

"I'm Brannoc," he said. "Prospector. Looking for a vein of ore that sings wrong."

"Ore does not sing," Corwin said.

"This one does. Or it did. Or it's supposed to. Heard about it from a cousin who heard about it from a cousin who heard about it from a miner who swore he heard it singing in the deep. Wrong singing. The wrongness drew him in. The drawing-in killed him. And the killing made me want to find it — because if it killed him, it's worth finding. And if it's worth finding, I'm the dwarf to find it."

He said this with the particular cheerfulness dwarves use when discussing things that have killed their relatives. It was not cheerfulness at all — it was defiance. *You took him but you won't take me,* the cheerfulness said. *I will find the thing that killed him and I will look at it and I will walk away, and the walking away will prove I am stronger than it was.*

I understood. I had done the same with Fenn. Gone to the grove, night after night, spoken his name to the bark. Defiance. *You took him but you won't take his name.*

I did not say any of that. I said, "Can we come with you."

Brannoc looked at me. Then Corwin. Then the empty bowls. Then back at me. "You already have."

---

The vein was three days' walk into the hills, down a track that was not a track but a suggestion — a line of disturbed grass and broken stone that said someone had walked this way before and the walking had not been easy.

Brannoc talked as we walked. He talked about ore and stone, about the way rock holds memory the way my people hold it in voice.

"Stone remembers," he said. "That's the thing your people don't understand, elf. You think memory is a thing you do with your mouth. But stone remembers too. Stone holds the hands that shaped it, the voices that sang over it, the grief that was poured into it when the one who shaped it died. Stone is the oldest keeper in the world. Older than your trees. Older than your songs. Older than your Quiet."

I looked at him. He was looking at the hills, at the rock that came close to the surface, at the stone that was the bones of the world. I understood, in that moment, that the keeping was not something the Nullwood had invented. The keeping was something the world had always done — in stone and in voice and in cloth tied to posts. The Quiet was the aberration. The aberration was what I had been exiled for refusing. And the refusing had brought me here: to a dwarf who listened to stone, a man who hunted old songs, and a road that went south toward a ruin called the Hollow Choir.

---

We found the mine on the fourth day. A hole in the hillside, old, collapsed in places, the timbers rotting and the track rusted and the whole of it smelling of damp and ore and the particular smell of a place that has been abandoned and is not glad of the company.

Brannoc lit a lantern. Corwin checked his blade. I stood at the mouth of the mine and looked into the dark. The old fear rose — the fear of the enclosed, the fear of the Nullwood elf who had grown up under open canopy and was not yet used to a ceiling where the sky should be. It pressed down. My breath went short. My heart went fast. My body wanted to run. I did not run. The not-running was something I had learned on the road, and the learning let me walk into the mine behind Brannoc and Corwin, into the dark that was not dark but only unfamiliar.

We went down. The track sloped. The air grew cool. The lantern threw shadows — hard, sharp shadows cast by rock, not the soft shifting shadows of the wood. The eye saw things that were not there. The mind worked harder than it should.

We walked for an hour. The track ended at a chamber, wide, low-ceilinged, the walls veined with ore that caught the lantern-light and threw it back in dull flashes. Brannoc stopped. He held up his hand. "Listen."

We listened.

A singing. Low, faint, coming from the far wall, from the vein itself. Not a sound but a vibration. Wrong. The kind of wrong that makes the skin prickle. Something was singing in the stone, and it should not have been there.

---

It came.

The wall burst. Stone and dust showered. Through the gap came a thing — all claws and teeth and segmented body, the hunter of the deep. The wrong-singing ore had drawn it, and it had been waiting.

Brannoc swore. Corwin drew his blade. I pressed against the wall.

The thing was fast. It was on Brannoc before he had his axe up. Claws came down. Brannoc rolled. Claws hit stone. Sparks. The thing screamed — a high thin vibration, the same wrongness as the ore. My teeth ached.

It came at Corwin from behind. I shouted. He turned. His blade met the claws.

Brannoc was up. Axe in hand. He swung. The axe bit into the thing's side. It screamed and turned. Corwin was on it — blade in its back. They had it between them.

It was strong. Armored. The armor turned blades. Brannoc swore again, swung again, missed. Then I saw it, in the lantern-light — the joints. Where the segments met. Where the armor was thin.

I shouted. Not a word. Not a name. A sound — the sound of the Nullwood, the sound that dampens, that quiets. The thing paused. Brannoc swung into the joint. The axe bit deep. Corwin drove his blade into the other joint.

The thing went still.

---

We stood in the chamber, breathing hard. The lantern flickered. The thing lay dead at our feet.

Brannoc looked at me. "What in the name of the Stonefather was that."

"I don't know," I said. "I just shouted."

"You shouted it still. You shouted it quiet. You shouted it into a thing that was moving too fast to be quieted, and you quieted it." He looked at Corwin. "Your elf's got a voice that does things."

Corwin looked at me. "I have seen that before. The Nullwood's gift. The dampening. It is not just in the trees. It is in the people."

I looked at the thing at our feet. The gift was not something I had left behind in the Nullwood. I had carried it out with me, onto the road, into the mine, into the fight. And it had saved us.

---

We came out of the mine into the late afternoon, into the sky and the wind and the open country. Brannoc led us to a standing stone at the edge of the mine, old, weathered, the kind of stone dwarves set at the mouths of their mines to mark the dead — to keep their names in the stone the way the stone keeps all things, patiently, without forgetting.

Brannoc laid his hand on the stone. He closed his eyes. He listened.

Then he went quiet.

Not the quiet of the Nullwood. Not the quiet of the fold. A different quiet — the quiet of surprise, of confusion. Something that should be there was not. Or something that should not be there was. Either way, it did not make sense.

He opened his eyes. He looked at Corwin. He looked at me. "There's a hole."

"What do you mean," Corwin said.

"In the stone's memory. A place where someone should be and isn't. Or a place where someone was so long ago the stone has forgotten — but stone doesn't forget. It can't. Forgetting shouldn't be possible. And yet here it is."

He looked at Corwin. He looked at him the way you look at a thing that does not make sense, that should not be, that is a hole in the world.

Then he grinned. He clapped Corwin on the shoulder. "Well, you're a strange one, human. Stranger than I thought. But you fight well and you don't complain about the ale, so I'll walk with you anyway."

And he walked with us. We were a trio now. The road ahead was the rest of the song.

But I saw it. The look he gave Corwin when he thought Corwin was not looking. The unease. The thing that did not make sense. And I filed it, the way I filed all things — in the place where I kept the small impossibilities, the things that did not add up, the things that would one day, when there were enough of them, make a picture.

But that is the rest of the song. This was still the beginning. Still the road, and the trio, and the dwarf who listened to stone, and the man who hunted old songs, and the girl who had walked out of the Nullwood into the loud, into the world, into the story.
