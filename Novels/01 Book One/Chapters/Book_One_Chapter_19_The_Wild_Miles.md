# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Chapter Nineteen — The Wild Miles

---

We heard the rumors at a crossroads. They came the way rumors always come — in fragments, from travelers passing through, each adding their weight to the thing they carried.

Dragons binding to mortals by sacred pact, they said. Gods taking sides in open war. A war kindling in the south, far enough to ignore, close enough to feel in the ground underfoot.

Brannoc listened with the particular attention dwarves give to stories. "Heard it before," he said. "My grandfather told me. It's been happening for a long time."

But Corwin listened differently. His stillness deepened. The door behind his eyes opened a crack, and I saw him remembering — not hearing for the first time, but knowing from before. The difference mattered.

"I have seen it," he said. "Mortals binding dragons. Gods taking sides. Wars kindling and burning and dying and kindling again."

The deflection was smooth. Almost too smooth. I let it go. I filed it with the other impossibilities.

---

The wild miles were wild. The land was old and untended, full of the things that grow when no one is watching.

Wren led us. She read the land the way I read memory. She saw the dangers before we reached them — the soft ground, the old dens, the places where the territory of something larger overlapped the path.

"There's something here," she said. "Something old. Territorial. The kind of thing that haunts the old places."

She threaded us through for three days. She was fearless. She was fast. She read the ground the way other people read a page.

On the fourth day, it found us.

---

It came out of the ruins. Old. Large. Hungry. Built like a bear and moved like a cat, all muscle and speed and the wrongness of a predator that had lived long enough to learn patience.

Maelis drew first. The arrow sank into its shoulder. The thing barely slowed.

Brannoc met it with his axe. The blade bit deep. The thing swiped. Brannoc went down, rolled, came up cursing.

Wren darted in. Knife low and fast, aiming for the soft places. It turned. She dodged. Barely.

Corwin stepped in front of us. Blade up. Stance set.

The thing hit him. It hit him hard. Its claw caught him across the middle — through his guard, through his side, through. A wound that should have ended any man who ever lived.

He did not fall.

He did not die.

He shifted his weight and drove his blade into the thing's throat. It went down. It stayed down.

We stood in the ruins, breathing hard. The thing dead at our feet. And Corwin standing.

Wren went to him. She went the way you go to blood, to hurt, to the urgency of a wound that should be fatal. She peeled back his shirt.

Then she stopped.

The wound was closing. Not slowly. Not the way a wound closes when it is healing right. It was knitting itself shut, fast and clean, the torn edges drawing together like a seam pulled tight.

She looked at him. "This is not right."

"Wounds do not linger on me," he said. "They never have."

"This is not how bodies work."

He met her eyes. The stillness was there, and the carefulness, and the warmth. He said, "It signifies nothing."

She looked at the wound. A pink line across his ribs where a hand-span of torn flesh had been.

She looked at her hands. She was shaken.

Brannoc watched from a few paces away. The same look he had given Corwin at the standing stone — the look that had been unease then and was certainty now. He had known. All along. And now he knew for sure.

He said nothing.

---

We walked on. Bloodied but through. Wren guided us, quieter than before, her eyes on Corwin when she thought no one was looking.

The Hollow Choir was close. I could feel it. We were within days of the oldest song, and Corwin was closer to it than he had been in lifetimes.

But that is the rest of the song.
