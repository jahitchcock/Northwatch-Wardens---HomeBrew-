# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Chapter Thirty — The Old Songs

---

The practice spread.

Not quickly — the Nullwood does not move quickly. Not easily — the Quiet had been the law for two generations, and laws do not dissolve in a single speaking. But the names had been spoken, and the speaking could not be un-spoken, and the people who had heard the names carried them home the way you carry a coal from a fire — carefully, close to the chest, knowing it could warm you or burn you and choosing warmth.

Ilvath spoke Caelen's name again. And again. The third time, he wept. The fourth time, he smiled. The fifth time, he asked me to help him remember the details — the maps, the drawings, the way his son had laughed with his whole body.

Maerin spoke Fenn's name every morning. She spoke it to the grove where he had played. She spoke it to the tree where he had carved his animals. She spoke it to me, and I kept it, and the keeping was not a hoarding but a sharing, and the sharing made the grief survivable.

Niamh became the second Keeper. She had the gift — the ear for detail, the patience for listening, the voice that could hold a name without breaking it. She learned the practice the way Maelis had learned it: by watching, by trying, by failing, by trying again. She spoke the names of the Withering dead, one by one, and the grove listened, and the Quiet gave way.

---

The order took shape.

Not as a council. Not as a tribunal. Not as the kind of institution that enforces and governs and decides. The Memory-Keepers were a practice, not a power. They were the people who spoke the names aloud so that nothing true was lost. They were the ones who sat with the grieving and helped them find the words. They were the ones who kept the songs.

I was the first. I would not be the last.

The Nullwood changed. The Quiet gave way to the Speaking — not everywhere, not all at once, but in grove after grove, in hollow after hollow, in the small daily acts of a people learning to grieve aloud. The architecture of silence did not collapse. It was simply abandoned, room by room, as the people moved out of the house and into the open air.

The Verdant Mother had taught us, the old songs said, that you do not take a home from the world; you let the world grow you one. The Nullwood was growing a new home. A home with doors.

---

Corwin was gone.

He had walked east, toward the road that had no end, and the road had taken him the way roads take wanderers — silently, completely, without the courtesy of a destination. I did not know where he was. I did not know if he was alive, though I suspected he was, because death had not yet found a way to hold him and I did not believe it ever would.

But he returned.

Not in person — not in the first years. In other ways. In the stories that reached me from the frontier: a man who had walked through a reaver camp and come out the other side without a scratch. A stranger who had sat with a dying woman in a border village and sung her across with a melody no one had heard before. A wanderer who had appeared at a crossroads, helped a lost child find the road home, and vanished before anyone could ask his name.

I kept the stories. I spoke them aloud. I added them to the keeping, the way you add a verse to a song that has no ending.

He returned, across the long years, the way he always would. He came back to the Nullwood — not often, not regularly, but at the moments when the world turned and the ages shifted and the old songs needed a witness. He came back and sat beside me in the grove and told me things I did not yet understand, and I kept them, and the keeping was the bond, and the bond endured.

*"He would return to me across the whole of my life. Each time, he would be older in his eyes and younger in his face, and each time I would be older in both, and the gap between us would narrow until it was almost nothing, and almost nothing was enough."*

---

I am old now.

The Nullwood has grown around me. The Memory-Keepers number in the dozens — men and women, elves and visitors from the frontier, anyone who has lost someone and needs to speak the name aloud. The practice has spread beyond the Nullwood, carried by travelers and traders and the slow migration of a discipline that answers a need older than any law.

Maelis's students in Thistlebrook have become Keepers of their own. Brannoc's people in Tharundor have adapted the practice to stone — they carve the names into the walls of the deep holds, and the stone remembers the way stone remembers everything. The coastal peoples have woven it into their tide-songs. The Emberlands folk have made it part of their fire-rites.

The old songs are being spoken across the world. And the speaking is the keeping. And the keeping is the living.

---

I sit in the grove.

The same grove where I whispered Fenn's name to a tree, twenty years and a lifetime ago. The same bark. The same roots. The same light filtering through the canopy in dappled patterns that shift with the wind.

I am alone. The grove is quiet — not the Quiet, the other quiet, the natural quiet of a forest at rest. The birds are singing. The leaves are moving. The world is going on being the world.

I hum.

Not deliberately. Not with intention. The melody comes out of me the way breath comes — unbidden, automatic, the kind of thing the body does without consulting the mind. A few notes. A fragment. A scrap of tune that has lived in me so long I have forgotten where I learned it.

It is Wren's tune.

The one Corwin hummed on the road, the one he sang to her in the vigil, the one she hummed in her fever dreams — the ancient melody, the oldest song, the thing from before. I never learned it. No one taught it to me. I heard it once, in a camp in the wild miles, from the lips of a dying woman and the voice of a man who had loved her across ages.

And yet I carry it.

I do not know why. I do not know how. I only know that the melody is inside me the way the names are inside me — kept, spoken, given away to the air and the trees and the silence that is not the Quiet.

The song outlives the woman. The song outlives the age. The song will return, on other lips, in other ages, in the mouths of women who do not know they are carrying it and men who have chased it across the whole of time.

These are the old songs. And this was the first.
