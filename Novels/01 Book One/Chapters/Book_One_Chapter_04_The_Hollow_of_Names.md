# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Chapter Four — The Hollow of Names

---

I have not yet told you the worst thing I did in those years, because I have been afraid of how it will look set down beside everything else, and because I have spent a long time learning that it was not the brave thing I believed it to be when I did it. But the song does not go on without it. So.

There was a hollow, north and west of the grove, where two great roots of a fallen elder-tree had lifted and crossed and made a chamber under the earth, dry and dark and forgotten, in a part of the wood the Thornwatch did not walk because nothing lived there worth the watching. I had found it as a child, the way children find the one place no one else knows. And when I was old enough to understand what my people had done to their dead, and young enough to think I could undo it alone, I had begun to fill it.

Not with things. With names.

I want to be careful here, because the keeping I did in the hollow was real magic, the true old art, and I will not have you think it was a child's game of pretend. Our gift was never in the tongue alone; it was in the wood, in the made thing, in the marriage of the said and the kept. The old keepers tied their name-cords. I had no cord and no one to teach me, so I taught myself, badly, in the dark, out of the relics in the case and the half-songs no one had quite managed to forbid. I carved. I took slips of heartwood and I cut a name into each one — not the letters, we had letters but that was not the way, I mean I cut the *shape* of the saying, the way a name moves in the mouth, so that to run your thumb along the grain was to be made to speak it — and then I spoke it, aloud, alone, the whole small life of the one it held as much as I had been able to gather, and I laid the slip in the hollow with the others.

There were a great many others. That is the thing I need you to see. I had been doing this for years, since before the night this song began, and I had not kept only my own dead. I had gone gathering. I had sat with the old ones at their candlelit windows and let them think I was only keeping them company, and I had listened, and I had stolen — there is no gentler word — the names and the faces and the small true histories they let slip in their unguarded hours, the husband, the daughter, the whole drowned village the Withering had taken, all the dead our people had agreed to unspeak, and I had carried them down into the dark under the crossed roots and cut them into the wood and said them, one by one, so that somewhere in the silent Nullwood there was one place, one, where the dead were still being spoken.

I thought it was the most loving thing anyone in the whole wood was doing. I thought I was the only one keeping faith.

I have had ages to look at that hollow with an older eye, and I will tell you what I see now, though it shames me. I see a girl who could not bear that her people had buried their dead in silence — and who answered it by burying them again, in a hole in the ground, where no living soul but herself would ever hear a single name she had saved. I had not freed those names. I had taken them out of one dark and put them in another that only I held the key to. I had made myself the sole keeper of a hoard, and I had called it love, the way my elders made themselves the sole keepers of a silence and called it mercy. We were doing the same thing, my people and I. We were both of us holding the dead so tightly that no one else could reach them. The only difference was that I held them speaking and they held them still, and I was so proud of that difference, so certain it was everything, that it took me half my life and the loss of nearly everyone in this song to understand it was almost nothing at all.

A thing kept is not a thing saved. I told you that on the first page. This is where I should have learned it, in the hollow, surrounded by the hoard of my own keeping, and did not. Some lessons will not be learned until they are paid for.

---

The night after the Severing — for I could not stay away after that, after the weaver, after the stranger and his question I had been unable to answer — I went down into the hollow to do the thing I had never let myself do.

I went to keep Fenn.

I had carried his slip uncut for two years. I want you to understand what that means: I, who had stolen the names of strangers and cut them into the wood without a tremor, had not been able to make myself cut my own brother's. Because the others I knew only as gathered things, as stories handed across a windowsill, and Fenn I knew as Fenn. To cut his name was to admit that he needed keeping. That he was gone far enough to require it. As long as the slip stayed blank in my hand I could pretend he was only away, only in the next room, only standing in a doorway with all the time in the world. The blank wood was the last place he was still alive.

That night I cut it.

I sat in the dark under the crossed roots with one foxfire lamp dimmed to almost nothing and I cut my brother's name into the heartwood, the shape of the saying, the way *Fenn* rises and stops in the mouth, and my hands were not steady the way the keepers' hands were always steady, and the cut went crooked twice and I did not start again because a crooked keeping is still a keeping and he would have laughed at it, he would have laughed at the crookedness and made me laugh too. And when it was cut I held it in both hands and I said him. All of him. The unfinished laugh and the doorway and the way he had carried me on his shoulders through the lower terraces when I was small and named the trees for me with names he invented, none of them true, all of them better than true. I said the morning he took sick and the long bad season and the last clear hour at the end when he had known us all and been afraid and let us see it, which was the bravest thing, to let us see it. I said it aloud, in the dark, to no one, the whole of my brother, for the first time since the Quiet took him — and I wept, not the silent allowed weeping of the rows but the other kind, the loud kind, the kind we had unlearned, and it tore out of me and filled the little chamber and I did not stop it.

Which is how I did not hear her come.

---

Niamh had followed me. She told me, after, that she had followed me for fear — that she had seen my face since the Severing and known I was going to do something that would end me, and had meant only to catch me at the boundary and turn me back, the way she had turned me back from the case a hundred times. She had not known about the hollow. No one had known about the hollow. That was the whole of its safety, and I had broken it myself, that night, with the one thing I had never done there before: I had made a sound loud enough to follow.

I looked up and she was in the mouth of the chamber with the dark behind her, and her eyes were going over the hollow — over the slips, the years of them, the hundreds of cut names laid in their rows under the crossed roots, the whole forbidden hoard of the spoken dead — and I watched her understand it. I watched it land on her face, all of it, what it was, what it meant, what it would cost. And I was not afraid, in that first moment. I was glad. That is the terrible thing I have to tell you. After all the years alone in the dark, the very first feeling I had when someone finally saw my hoard was *gladness* — at last, at last, someone will know — and I have wondered ever since whether some part of me followed me into that hollow as surely as Niamh did, some part that had wanted to be found, that had made the sound on purpose, that was tired to death of keeping the keeping a secret. I will never know. The heart that wants to be seen does not announce itself to the heart that wants to hide.

"Elowen," she said. Only my name. And then she was across the chamber with her hands on my arms, not looking at the slips now but at me, and her face was not the horror I had braced for — it was worse, it was fear, the old fear, the love-shaped fear, gone enormous. "What is this," she said, "what *is* this, what have you — how long," and then, before I could answer, "they will not exile you for this. Do you understand me. This is not the case, this is not holding a cord too long, this is — Elowen, they will not *exile* you for this." And she did not say what they would do instead, and she did not have to, and we stood there in the hoard of the dead with that unsaid thing between us, the two of us who had spent our whole lives learning what could not be said.

I should have calmed her. I see that now. I should have taken her hands and been steady and small and made it nothing, the way she had made me nothing a hundred times in the bright mornings. But I was raw from keeping my brother and I was glad to be seen and I was, under all of it, still arrogant — still so sure I was the one doing right — and instead of calming her I tried to *show* her. I picked up a slip, one of the gathered ones, an old man's drowned daughter, and I pressed it into her hand and I told her to run her thumb along it, to feel it make her say the name, to understand what we had been robbed of. I tried to give her the gift. In a hole in the ground, over a hoard, with the wood not yet cold from my weeping, I tried to make my frightened friend into another keeper.

And Niamh — gentle Niamh, who had covered for me her whole life — looked at the slip in her hand and at the rows of the dead all around her and at me, and something in her broke. Not toward me. For me. She put the slip down as though it had burned her and she said, "I can't. I can't carry this. Elowen, I can't be the only one who knows this and watches them not know," and she was weeping now too, and I understood, far too late, what I had done. I had not given her a gift. I had given her my hoard. I had made her, in one stroke, the sole other keeper of a secret that could end me — had laid on her exactly the weight I had carried alone for years, the unbearable weight of knowing alone — and she was not built for it, she had never been built for it, she was built to love openly and live in the light, and I had dragged her down into my dark and locked it onto her and called it a gift.

She did not betray me. I have spent ages making certain that word never attaches to her name, in any hall, in any telling, and I will not let it attach here. She did not go to the elders. What she did was smaller and more human and more impossible to forgive myself: she could not carry it, and it showed. She went back up into the village with my secret on her face like a wound she did not know how to hide, and she was clumsy with fear in a place where everyone had spent four hundred years reading the smallest tremor of a folded thing, and within two days the keepers had looked at Niamh's frightened face and at the nights I had gone missing and at the case I held too long, and they had done what our people were better at than any people who ever lived. They had read the silence. And the silence said: *the girl is keeping something.*

---

They came for the hollow on the third night.

I was not in it. That is the only mercy in the whole of this part, and it was not meant as a mercy; it was only chance, that I was at home pretending to sleep when the Thornwatch found the crossed roots and the chamber under them and the hoard. I learned it from the sound, which is a thing I have never forgotten — that in a village that had made silence into its god, the thing that told me my life was over was *noise.* Movement in the dark terraces. Doors. The low urgent voices of people who have found a thing so far outside the order of the world that even the Quiet cannot hold against it. I lay in my small dark and listened to my people make sound for the first time in my life, and I knew exactly what they had found, and I got up and dressed in the dark with steady hands, because there was nothing left to be afraid of. The thing I had been afraid of for years had finally happened, and I found, as you sometimes do, that the far side of the worst fear is a strange flat calm.

They brought me before the keepers in the grey of the morning, in the keeper-school, in the ring where I had been taught to fold. They had laid the slips out on the long case — all of them, the whole hoard, hundreds of names carried up out of my dark and set in rows in the light — and I think they meant the sight of it to break me. To show me the magnitude of what I had done, the way they showed children the swollen river. Ilvath stood at the head of them, and his face was the face of a man looking at the end of something he had given his life to, and the other keepers stood around the case with the slips between us, and they waited for me to be ashamed.

I looked at my hoard laid out in the morning light, every name I had stolen and saved and spoken, my brother's crooked slip among them where they had found it in my own hands two nights before — and I was not ashamed.

I have thought a great deal about why. It was not courage; I have told you, courage came later. It was not even defiance, not really. It was that they had laid out, on that case, in rows, in the open light of day, four hundred years of our unspoken dead — more names said in one place than the Nullwood had heard aloud in two generations — and they thought they were showing me my crime, and what I saw was the most beautiful thing I had ever made. They had gathered my whole keeping into one place and shown it to me whole, which I had never been able to do myself, and I looked at it and I felt my chin come up.

And Ilvath saw it. He saw that I was not ashamed. I watched him see it, watched it reach him across the case and the rows of the dead, and I understood — I did not have the words for it then, but I understood — that this was the thing that frightened them. Not the hollow. Not the hoard, terrible as they found it. A girl can be caught at a crime and be ashamed and be forgiven; shame is how the Quiet healed its own breaks, how it folded the breaker back into the fold. But I stood in the ring with my stolen dead laid out between us and my chin up and no shame anywhere in me, and what I gave them instead of shame was the one thing their whole world had no answer for.

I gave them a girl who thought she was right.

And I saw, in Ilvath's old grieving face, the moment he understood that there would have to be a trial. Not because of what I had done. Because of what I would not feel about it.
