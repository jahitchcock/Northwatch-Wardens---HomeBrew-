# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Chapter Six — The Threshold

---

The mist held me for the first hour. That is the thing I remember most clearly about the leaving — not the fear, not the grief, not the enormity of what I had done, but the mist. The Nullwood's mist is not like the mist of the lowlands; it is a living thing, a breath the wood exhales at its edges, and it had been the wall of my whole world for as long as I could remember, and now I was walking through it the way you walk through a curtain, and on the other side the world was a different world entirely.

The trees thinned first. Not gradually, the way they thin at the edges of any wood, but in the Nullwood's particular way — a sudden opening, a releasing, as though the great trees had been holding their ranks and now, at the stones, they let go, and the canopy broke apart, and the sky came down. I had not known how low the sky was. In the Nullwood the sky was a thing you glimpsed in pieces, a blue shard through the leaves, a star caught between branches, and I had grown up believing the world was roofed. Now the roof was gone and the sky was enormous and it pressed down on me with a weight I had not expected, a weight that was not grief but was near to it — the weight of a world without a ceiling, a world that went on and on in every direction and did not care whether you walked through it or not.

The road was real. That surprised me too. I had half expected the path to end at the stones, the way the Nullwood's paths ended, swallowed back into the root and the loam, but the lowland road came up to the boundary and kept going, a rutted track of packed earth and gravel that ran down through the mist and out into a country I had no name for. There were wheel-marks in it. There were boot-prints. Someone had walked this road, recently, in the damp, and the marks were still fresh, and I stood at the edge of everything I had ever known and looked at the marks and thought: *people live out there. People who are not my people. People who do not keep the Quiet.*

And then I walked on, because there was nothing else to do, and the mist thinned, and the world opened, and I came down out of the Nullwood into the loud.

---

I have tried, many times, to set down what the loud world was like to a girl who had never known anything but silence, and I have never quite managed it, because the thing that made it overwhelming was not any single sound but the *density* of sound, the way the air outside the Nullwood was thick with noise the way water is thick with current. Birds. Wind in grass — not in trees, in *grass*, a sound I had never heard, a dry whispering ripple that went on and on and did not stop the way the wood's sounds stopped, did not hush itself, did not fold. Insects. A stream somewhere, running over stones, the water speaking its own name in a voice that carried and did not care who heard it. And under all of it, the sound of the world simply *being* — the creak of wood, the shift of earth, the distant lowing of something large and animal that I would learn, later, was a cow.

A cow. I had heard of them. I had never heard one. The sound of it reached me across the open country like a word in a language I did not speak, and I stopped on the road and listened to it, and I felt something move in me that was not grief and not fear and not exhilaration but all three at once, tangled together, the feeling of a girl standing at the edge of a world so much larger and louder and more alive than anything she had imagined that the imagining itself felt, in retrospect, like another kind of Quiet.

The first people I met were at a crossroads, half a day's walk from the stones. A cart, a horse, a man and a woman in roughspun clothes the color of the road, their faces brown and broad and open in a way no elven face was open, their voices carrying to me before I could see them — talking, *talking*, as they worked, the way humans talk, filling the air with sound the way my people filled it with silence, and I stood at the edge of the crossroads and watched them and felt the shock of it the way you feel cold water — not painful, exactly, but so far outside what the body expects that the body does not know what to do with it.

They saw me. The woman called out — something friendly, something I did not catch, the trade-tongue I had learned in the keeper-school but had never heard spoken aloud by anyone who was not elven — and I answered, and my voice sounded strange to me, thin and careful in the open air, a voice made for the hush of the wood trying to carry across a field. They looked at me the way you look at a thing that does not quite belong — not unkindly, but with the particular attention that the frontier gives to anything out of place. An elf. Alone. On the lowland road, with nothing but a pack and the clothes on her back and the look of someone who has just walked out of a story.

The man asked where I was bound. I did not know. I said *south*, because south was the direction the road went, and because I had not yet learned that the frontier does not care about directions, only destinations. He nodded, the way you nod at a child who has said something simple, and the woman gave me a piece of bread, and I took it, and I ate it standing at the crossroads, and it was the first food I had eaten that had not been made by elven hands, and it was coarse and good and tasted of smoke and something I would learn was salt, and I ate it and I thanked them and I walked on, and behind me I heard them talking again, already, about me, their voices carrying in the open air the way sound carries when there is no wood to swallow it, and I understood, for the first time, what the Quiet had been protecting us from.

Not danger. Not exactly. *Being heard.* The Nullwood had made silence into a wall, and the wall had kept us safe from the world, and the world had kept itself safe from us, and now I was outside the wall and every sound I made went out into the open and did not come back, and I was, for the first time in my life, a thing that could be *found.*

---

I was marked before I knew it. That is the thing about the frontier — it reads you faster than you read it, and it reads you for the things you do not know you are showing. I was young. I was elven, which on the frontier meant strange and valuable and possibly worth something to the right buyer. I was alone, which meant no one was coming for me. And I walked the way the Nullwood had taught me to walk — quietly, carefully, as though the ground might hear me — which to the frontier eye was the walk of someone who had never had to run, and who would not know how to.

The crossroads man had looked at me too long. I understood that later. Not with malice — with the particular attention a man gives to a thing he might tell someone about, later, for a price. I was news. I was a story. And on the frontier, stories are currency, and currency attracts the kind of people who take it.

It was two days after the crossroads, at a place where three roads met in a tangle of ruts and a dead tree, and it was after dark, and I had made the mistake — the first of many — of stopping too close to the meeting of the roads, in the shelter of a stone wall that was not a wall but the remains of one, and I had not made a fire because I did not yet know how, and I was sitting in the dark with my pack and my bread and the last of my courage, when I heard them.

Three of them. I heard them before I saw them, because the Nullwood had taught me to listen the way other people are taught to breathe, and the sounds they made were not the sounds of travelers — travelers make a certain kind of noise, the noise of people going somewhere, and these men were making the noise of people looking for something, the slow careful noise of hunters who have caught a scent. I went very still. I made myself part of the stone wall the way I had made myself part of the tree-shadow in the grove, and I breathed against my sleeve, and I waited.

They came out of the dark on the far side of the dead tree. I saw them by the starlight — three shapes, rough, armed, moving with the easy confidence of men who have done this before and expect to do it again. Road-reavers. I did not know the word then; I would learn it, and I would learn to hate it, in the weeks that followed. They were brigands, the frontier's particular cruelty, men who hunted lone travelers the way the Thornwatch hunted the grove — systematically, patiently, with the quiet efficiency of people who have made predation into a trade.

One of them had a lantern. Shuttered, the way the Thornwatch carried theirs, and the irony of it — the same light, the same purpose, the same patient searching — was not lost on me even then, young and terrified as I was. The lantern-slit swung across the dead tree and the ruts and the remains of the wall, and I pressed myself flatter and breathed smaller and thought, with the whole of my frightened heart, *not again, not again, please not again* — as though the world owed me a second mercy it had never promised me a first.

The light passed. Passed and swung back. Passed again. And then one of them said something — low, a word I did not catch — and the light came toward me, and I knew, the way you know a thing in the body before the mind has named it, that this time the light would not swing away.

I ran.

---

I have told this part of the song in many halls, and I have always told it honestly, which means I have always told it badly, because there is no way to make running sound brave. I ran. I ran the way a rabbit runs, without thought, without plan, into the dark and the brush and the broken ground beyond the dead tree, and behind me I heard them shout — not angry, not even surprised, *amused*, the sound of men who have seen this before and know how it ends — and I ran harder, because the amusement was worse than the anger, the amusement was the sound of people who were certain they would catch me, and I was not yet ready to be caught.

I do not remember the running clearly. I remember pieces. The scratch of brush. The give of soft ground under my boots. A fall — I fell, I know I fell, because I came up with my hands full of dirt and my knee singing and the sound of them closer — and then a ditch, a deep one, the kind the frontier cuts for drainage, and I threw myself into it and lay flat in the cold muck at the bottom and pulled the dead grass over me and held my breath and held my heartbeat and held everything, the way the Nullwood had taught me to hold, the one gift the Quiet gave me that I would not unlearn.

They came to the edge of the ditch. I heard their boots on the lip above me. I heard them talking, low, and the lantern-light came down into the muck and played across the dead grass a hand's breadth from my face, and I lay in the cold and the wet and the dark and I did not move, and I thought of the grove, and I thought of the Thornwatch, and I thought: *I have done this before. I have lain in the dark while men with lanterns searched for me. The only difference is that this time I am not in my own wood, and this time they are not looking for a girl who spoke a name, and this time they will not swing away.*

They swung away.

I do not know why. Chance. Luck. The particular mercy of a ditch that was deep enough and a girl who was quiet enough and a night that was dark enough. They moved on, their boots fading down the road, their voices carrying and then not carrying, and I lay in the muck for a long time after, shaking, my knee throbbing, my pack gone — I had dropped it in the running, the bread, the few things I had carried out of the Nullwood, all of it gone, left behind in the dark the way you leave behind anything that slows you down when the thing behind you is faster than the thing ahead — and I was alone, and I was alive, and I was in a ditch in a country I did not know, with nothing, and the enormity of it came down on me the way the sky had come down on me at the stones, a weight I had not expected and did not know how to carry.

---

I wept. Not the silent weeping of the rows. Not the loud weeping of the hollow. Something else — something I had never done, something the Nullwood had never allowed and the loud world had never required, a weeping that was not grief and not relief but both at once, the weeping of a girl who has just learned that the world has teeth and that she has none, and that she is alive anyway, and that the being-alive is not a comfort but a fact, a plain hard fact she will have to do something with.

And then I did the thing I had not done since the night of the Severing, since the hollow, since the trial. I spoke.

I spoke my grief aloud. Not to anyone. Not for anyone. I spoke it to the dark and the ditch and the open sky above me, the sky that went on and on and did not care, and I said Fenn's name, and I said the word *home*, and I said the word *mother*, and I said the word *exile*, and I said them all aloud, in a voice that was not steady and not careful and not folded, and I waited for the thing that always came — the hand on the arm, the hush, the gentle correction, the *stop.*

Nothing came.

The dark held it. The ditch held it. The sky held it and gave nothing back, the way the bark had held Fenn's name and given nothing back, except that this was not the bark and this was not the grove and this was not the Quiet's particular cruelty of taking what you gave and making it smaller. This was the world, the loud world, the world that did not fold and did not hush and did not care what you said or how loudly you said it, and I lay in the muck at the bottom of a ditch and I spoke my grief into the open air and I discovered, for the first time in my life, that grief spoken aloud in a place that does not stop you is not a thing that diminishes. It is a thing that *moves.* It goes out of you and into the world and the world carries it, the way the wind carries a sound, and you are lighter for the carrying, not because the grief is less but because it is no longer yours alone.

That was the first free grief. I have had many since, and each one has been different, and none of them has been like that first one, the one in the ditch, the one that taught me what the Quiet had kept from me — that speaking is not the crime. That speaking is the gift. That the only crime is the silence that tells you speaking is the crime, and makes you believe it, and folds you so small around the believing that you cannot find the door of the house you have built around your own unspoken dead.

The Quiet had no power here. That was the thing I understood, lying in the muck with the sky above me and the world all around me and nothing between me and the dark but my own voice. The Quiet ended at the stones. Past the stones, the world was loud, and the loud was not cruel, and the loud was not kind, and the loud was only *loud*, and I was in it, and I was alive, and I was free, and the freedom was terrible, and the freedom was good, and I did not yet know which was which, and I would not know for a long time, and that was all right, because the not-knowing was itself a kind of freedom, the kind that comes when the worst thing has happened and you are still here, and the world is still here, and the world does not care, and the not-caring is the kindest thing the world has ever done for you.

I lay in the ditch until the stars came out, and then I climbed out, and I was cold, and I was alone, and I was hungry, and I was alive, and I began to walk, because there was nothing else to do, and the road went on, and I went on with it, and behind me the Nullwood held its silence, and ahead of me the world held its noise, and I was between them, and I was neither, and I was, for the first time, only myself.
