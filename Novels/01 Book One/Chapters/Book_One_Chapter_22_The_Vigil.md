# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Chapter Twenty-Two — The Vigil

---

The long night.

I do not mean a night of hours. I mean the long night — the vigil that has no end, the waiting that stretches past endurance. I mean the night where the only truth is that someone you love may not see the morning.

This was that night.

---

Wren drifted. Fever dreams. Fits of awareness.

In the first fit, she looked at Corwin with recognition. "Still here," she said. Not a question.

"Still here," he said.

In the second fit, she looked at me. "The song," she said. "I can hear it."

I did not know what she meant. But she began to hum.

---

The tune was wrong. Not wrong in the sense of a mistake — wrong in the sense that she could not have known it. The melody was ancient, older than the kingdoms, older than the First Age. The melody was from before.

I had not heard it. Corwin had not sung it. None of us had so much as whispered it in her presence.

But Wren hummed it. The ancient tune. The oldest song. She hummed it the way you hum a thing that has always been inside you.

---

Corwin went still.

I saw his face change. The grief did not leave — the grief would not leave for a long time — but something else moved behind it. Recognition. Something larger than recognition. Awe.

He leaned close to her.

"Where did you learn that?"

Wren's eyes opened. She looked at him the way you look at someone when you are too tired for questions. "It is in my head." A pause. "It has always been in my head."

The oldest song. The thing he had chased for centuries. The thing he had abandoned to stay with her. And it had been in her all along.

---

Corwin sang it back to her.

I heard the oldest song then. I heard what the Hollow Choir had pursued across ages. I heard the thing that had shaped the world and broken it and shaped it again.

And I heard it offered. Not hoarded. Not kept. Offered to a dying girl in a camp.

He laid it in her hands the way you lay a flower on a grave.

She was comforted.

---

The night stretched. The vigil continued.

Wren slept. Corwin held her hand. Maelis kept his own watch. I moved between them, keeping their griefs, keeping the long night in my memory so that I would not forget.

I remembered my brother, then. Fenn. I remembered his small hands and his serious face and the way he had said, *"When the worst thing happens, I will be your knife."*

And I understood something I had not understood before. The worst thing does not always arrive with a blade and a war-cry. Sometimes it arrives as a fever. Sometimes it arrives quietly. Sometimes the worst thing is ordinary.

The songs do not warn you about the ordinary griefs. But they are the ones that break you, in the end.

---

Dawn came.

I did not notice at first. The light crept across the ground, cold and pale. The trees took shape. The day returned.

Wren was still breathing.

It was not a grand moment. The songs would have made it dramatic — a final parting, a whispered goodbye, a hand loosening its grip in perfect light. But that is not how living things die. They drift. They linger. They hover on the threshold until the threshold decides.

She hovered.

The Song was planted.

It would not pay off for a long time — not until the Sundering, not until the origin, not until the oldest song was sung for the first time. But it was planted. And the planting was enough.

The night ended on the threshold of death.

Not past it.

Not across it.

Waiting.
