# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Chapter Five — The Quiet Judgment

---

They gave me three days. That was the mercy in it — the Quiet's particular mercy, which was always the cruelest kind, the kind that looked exactly like consideration. Three days to prepare my defense, three days in my own house, under my own roof, with the Thornwatch standing quiet at the end of the terrace and the whole village knowing and no one saying. Three days in which my mother and I lived inside the same silence we had always lived inside, except that now it had a shape, and the shape was the last of something.

I want to be honest about those three days, because the songs we tell of our trials are forever making them grander than they were. There was no grandeur in them. There was a girl in a house with her mother, and neither of them knew how to say what was happening, and so they did what our people did best — they moved around each other in the quiet, and they ate, and they slept or pretended to, and they let the hours pass the way you let a wound bleed into a cloth you will not look at.

Maerin did not ask me what I would say. I did not tell her. She mended, because her hands were never still, and I sat near her, and we did not speak of the thing that was coming, and in this way we kept our last days together the only way the Quiet allowed: as a shape we had both agreed not to see.

---

The trial was held in the keeper-school, in the ring where I had been taught to fold. That was deliberate. I understood it even then, young as I was — that they meant the place itself to do some of the work, the way a river does the work of the mill. The ring, the roots, the teaching-tree above us with its grey canopy filtering the morning into the soft green half-light we had all grown up inside — the whole architecture of the doctrine was the room, and I was to stand at its center and be reminded, by every surface, of what I had been taught and what I had refused.

The keepers sat in a half-circle on the roots. There were seven of them. I knew them all; they had been the faces of my whole education, the gentle voices that had taught me to let the dead go down, and now they looked at me with faces that were not gentle and not cruel but something harder to bear than either — they looked at me the way you look at a thing you love that has broken in a way you cannot mend. Ilvath sat at the center. He was the eldest. He would speak last. That was the way of it.

The charge was read by Keeper Thael, who had taught me the rite-words when I was small and had once, in a lesson on the name-cords, let me hold his hand while he showed me the knots, and had said, *this is what we were for, before.* He read it plainly, without heat, the way we did everything. That I, Elowen, daughter of Maerin, apprentice of the keeper-school, had for a span of years maintained a hidden archive of spoken names in violation of the Quiet; that I had gathered the names of the Withering dead without sanction and spoken them aloud in secret; that I had, on the night in question, been found in possession of the archive and had shown no repentance when confronted.

The last clause was the one that mattered, and we all knew it. The hoard was a crime. The hoard was survivable. A girl caught at a hoard could be shamed and folded back, the way a broken bone is set — painfully, but into the shape the body requires. What was not survivable was the clause at the end. *No repentance.* I had stood in the ring with my chin up and my dead laid out between us and I had given them nothing they could work with — no tears, no shame, no plea. I had given them a girl who thought she was right, and a people who had built their whole world on the proposition that she was wrong could not let that stand.

Thael finished reading. The ring was very quiet, even for us.

"You are given the floor," he said. "Speak in your defense."

---

I had not prepared a defense. I want you to know that. I had spent the three days not preparing, the way I had spent my whole life in the Nullwood not preparing for the moments that mattered, because preparation would have meant admitting they were coming, and I was not yet brave enough for that. I stood in the ring with the roots under my feet and the keepers before me and the whole weight of the Quiet pressing down from the canopy, and I opened my mouth, and what came out was not a defense.

It was a name.

I said his name. I said *Fenn*. Aloud. In the keeper-school. In the ring. Before the seven keepers and the teaching-tree and the whole gathered silence of my people. I said it the way it wanted to be said — not whispered, not hidden behind the teeth, not folded small and tucked away in the dark — but spoken, plainly, the way you say the name of someone you love to someone who might have loved them too.

*Fenn.*

The word landed in the room the way a stone lands in still water. I watched it move through them — through Thael, who closed his eyes; through old Aelith, who put her hand to her mouth; through the others, each in their own way receiving the blow of a name spoken where no name had been spoken in two generations. And I did not stop there. I could not have stopped if I had wanted to, because the thing I had held behind my teeth for years had found its way out and it would not go back, and I said more. I said what Fenn had been. I said the laugh that finished before the joke. I said the doorway and the shoulders and the names he gave the trees. I said the Withering and the last clear hour and the afraid. I said it all, standing in the ring, and I was weeping, the loud kind, the kind we had unlearned, and I did not care, because for the first time in my life I was doing what the gift was *for* — not folding, not burying, not tucking the dead into a place you cannot open, but *speaking* them, saying them, lending them a voice so they could go on a little longer in the only world that was left to them.

And the room reeled. I saw it. I saw the keepers look at each other with something I had never seen on their faces before — not anger, not even fear, but a kind of vertigo, the vertigo of people who have built a house on a foundation and are hearing, for the first time, the sound of the foundation cracking. Because what I was doing was not new. What I was doing was the oldest thing in the wood. I was keeping. I was doing the thing the keepers had been *for*, before the Quiet turned the word inside out, and every one of them knew it, and the knowing was the crack, and the crack was the sound, and the sound was the thing their whole world could not survive hearing.

I stopped. Not because I was finished. Because I had nothing left. I stood in the ring with my face wet and my hands open and my brother's name still hanging in the air like a bell-stroke that would not die, and I waited.

---

Ilvath spoke last. That was the way of it, and I had known it would be, and I had braced myself for it the way you brace for a thing you know is coming and cannot prevent and have decided, in the bracing, that you will bear.

He did not speak at once. He sat on the root with his old hands folded in his lap and he looked at me for a long time, and in his face I saw — I have had ages to read that face, and I see it still — the whole of the man. I saw the grief that had made him what he was, the wife and daughters the Withering had taken, the long years of teaching the rest of us to fold because he believed, truly believed, that it was the only mercy a people with our memory could bear. I saw the mornings he had watched me in the ring and known I was not folding and had let it pass, out of tenderness, out of the not wanting to be the one who breaks the thing. I saw the night he had voted, in the council of the keepers, to bring me to trial, and I saw — this is the part I have spent the longest time learning to hold — that he had wept while he did it.

"The charge is proven," he said. His voice was steady. It was always steady; he had made it steady the way a man makes a wall steady, by building it over the place that shakes. "The archive is real. The speaking is admitted. The absence of repentance is —" he paused, and I saw the pause cost him, saw it move through him like a thing swallowed against the grain — "noted."

He looked at me. Only me. The way he had looked at me in the ring on all those mornings, seeing what I was, seeing through the calm face to the open grief underneath.

"I have taught this girl," he said. "I have watched her grow in the art, and I have seen in her —" another pause, longer, the wall shaking — "a gift greater than any I have encountered in my years of keeping. She has a memory that holds the dead the way the wood holds rain, and she speaks them with a tenderness I have not heard since I was young, since before the Quiet, since the days when we still knew what the word *keeper* meant."

The room went very still. He had said too much, and he knew it, and he went on saying it anyway, because Ilvath was many things but he was not a liar, and he would not lie now, not here, not over me.

"And I believe," he said, "in the part of myself I have spent my life folding away — I believe she is not wrong about what we have done. I believe the Quiet has cost us more than we have named. I believe the old keeping was better than the new silence, and that we know it, all of us, and have agreed not to say it because the saying would break us."

He stopped. The other keepers were looking at him the way you look at a man who has set down a thing he has carried so long that the putting-down is itself a kind of breaking. And then he straightened, and the wall came back, and his voice went steady again, and he said the thing I had known he would say, the thing that was the whole of him — the grief and the duty and the love and the fear, all of it folded into a single word.

"I vote for exile."

---

It was four to three. Four for exile, three for a lesser sentence — confinement, restriction, a watching. Ilvath's vote was the one that carried it, because his was the eldest voice and because the others had watched his face while he spoke and had seen what it cost him and had understood that if the man who loved her most voted to send her away, then sending her away must be the right thing, must be the merciful thing, must be the only thing. That is how the Quiet worked, in the end. It did not need cruelty. It needed only that the gentlest person in the room believe, in the deepest part of himself, that gentleness required it.

The sentence was spoken by Thael, who read it from the old forms, the words that had been waiting in the case for a day like this, though no one living had expected the day to come. *You are released from the keeping of the Nullwood. You will go beyond the stones and you will not return. You will carry no name-cord and no slip and no relic of the art. You will go into the world and you will be loud there, and the wood will not hear you, and the Quiet will hold.*

*You will be loud there.* That was the cruelty of it, and they knew it, and they said it anyway, because the Quiet had a logic the way a wound has a logic — it could not allow even the punishment to be spoken gently, because gentleness would have been a crack, and the crack was the thing it could not survive. Go and be loud. Go and do elsewhere the thing we have forbidden here. Go and prove, by your going, that we were right to send you.

I stood in the ring and I heard it, and I did not weep, because the weeping had all gone out of me when I said my brother's name, and what was left was a flat clear thing, a calm I did not recognize as my own, the calm that comes when the worst thing has finally happened and you find you are still standing in it.

---

They gave me until the evening. That was the second mercy, and it was worse than the first, because the first mercy had been time and the second was the shape of the leaving. I was to go at dusk, by the north path, past the stones, and not return. No gathering. No rite. No Severing-tree raised for the living, because the Quiet had no form for the grief of losing someone who was not yet dead. I was to be unspoken the way the dead were unspoken — not with a rite but with an absence, a space where a person had been that the wood would close around and heal over, the way bark heals over a branch cut clean.

I went home. Maerin was at the cold hearth, and Fenn's cloak was on its peg, and she was mending, because her hands were never still, and I stood in the doorway and I looked at her, and she looked at me, and neither of us said anything, because the Quiet had taught us both, so thoroughly, so completely, that the things which matter most are the things you do not say — and now the thing that mattered most was that I was leaving, and she could not say it, and I could not say it, and the silence between us was the same silence it had always been, except that now it was the last one.

She set down her mending. She came to me. She put her arms around me, and I put mine around her, and we held each other in the doorway of the house we had built around our silence, and she did not weep, and I did not weep, and the cloak hung on its peg behind her and neither of us looked at it, and in this way we said goodbye the only way the Quiet allowed — as a shape we had both agreed not to see.

I have kept that embrace. Of all the things I have kept across all the years, I have kept the way my mother held me in the doorway without a sound, because in that holding I felt the whole of what the Quiet had done to her, what it had done to me, what it would take the rest of my long life to undo. She held me the way you hold a thing you are letting go of — tightly, and without looking at what you are doing, because looking would have broken the letting-go, and the letting-go was the only gift she had left to give me.

I left her there. I went up to my small dark and I gathered the few things I would carry, and I came down, and I passed the cloak on its peg, and I did not touch it, because touching it would have been a sound, and I was not yet free of the habit, and I went out into the evening by the north path, and the Thornwatch stood at the stones with their lanterns shuttered, and the wood thinned, and the canopy opened, and the grey stones came, and beyond them the mist and the lowland road and the whole wide loud world I had never known.

I did not look back. I want to be honest about that, too. The songs say I looked back, that I paused at the threshold and gazed upon the wood of my birth one last time, and it is a beautiful image and it is not true. I walked through the stones and I kept walking, because looking back would have been a kind of keeping, and I was not yet ready to keep anything. I was only ready to go.

And the Quiet held behind me, the way it always held, the way it would hold for two more generations until the day came that a Memory-Keeper walked back through the stones and spoke the names aloud in the grove where they had been buried, and the wood heard them, and the Quiet broke, and the old songs began again.

But that is the end of the song. This was still the leaving.

I walked out of the only world I had ever known, into the mist, into the loud, into the dark that was not dark but only unfamiliar, and I did not look back, and the stones passed behind me, and the wood closed, and the Quiet held, and I was alone.
