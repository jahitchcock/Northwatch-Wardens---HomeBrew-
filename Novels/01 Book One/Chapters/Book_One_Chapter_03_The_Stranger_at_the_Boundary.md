# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Chapter Three — The Stranger at the Boundary

---

There was a Severing the next day, and the whole of the village went out to it, because a Severing was the one occasion on which the Quiet stopped being a thing each of us kept alone and became a thing we made together, with our hands, in the open. We did not call it grief. We called it the giving of rest. But I have stood at the edge of human graves since, and heard them weep and say the name and throw the dark earth down, and I have come to think that what they do over their dead and what we did over ours were the same act turned inside out — they bury the body and keep the name; we kept the body in the wood forever and buried the name instead.

The dead one was a weaver called — but I will not write what she was called. That is the whole of what a Severing was for, and I will not undo it on a page, even now, even for her sake. She had died in the night, old and quiet, the good death our people are given when the Withering has finished with the world and left only the slow ordinary endings behind. They carried her out to the grove at first light, and the keepers raised her a tree.

You should understand that this was the beautiful part. I will not pretend it was not beautiful. The whole village came in their stillness and stood in the rows, and the keepers took the seedling — a memory-tree is planted living, the dead laid in at its roots so that the wood and the one it holds grow into each other across the years — and Ilvath spoke the rite, which is the only speaking the Quiet allows over a death, and which contains, by its design, no name at all.

I knew the rite by heart. We all did; it was the first thing the keeper-school taught and the last thing it took from us. *Go down into the long quiet. Be held and not held back. Let go your name as we let go our grief, and be at rest, and trouble the living no more.* That was the heart of it. *Trouble the living no more.* I had said those words a hundred times in the ring at Ilvath's knee and never once heard them, the way you can carry a stone in your pocket so long you forget it is a stone. I heard them that morning. I stood in the cold rows with my mother's shoulder a careful hand's breadth from my own, and I watched a whole people gather to make certain that a woman who had lived four hundred years would, by nightfall, be a thing none of them could ever say aloud again, and I heard the words for what they were, and something in me went very still and very cold and did not thaw for a long time after.

*Trouble the living no more.* We had made our dead into the troublers. We had taken the ones we loved and we had agreed, gently, all of us, by law, that the kindest thing we could do for them was forget how to speak them. And we called it rest. And we wept — for we did weep at a Severing, silently, the tears allowed where the words were not, which is the cruelest mercy I have ever been part of — and we went home lighter, and that was the horror of it, that it worked. That a thing so wrong could leave a grieving family lighter. I have never decided whether that made it more forgivable or less.

I did not fold anything that morning. I stood in the rows and I kept the weaver's name behind my teeth, the one I will not write, and I have it still, all these ages later, the last living keeping of a woman her own people unspoke. It is not much. It is a name and the fact that she wove. But it is more than the Quiet left her, and I have never been sorry I stole it.

---

It was during the rite that the stranger came to the boundary.

The Nullwood has no wall. It does not need one; the wood itself thickens at its edges into a thing no road runs through, and where the old paths come up out of the lowland they simply stop, at a line of grey stones the Thornwatch keep, and beyond the stones the trees close their ranks and the world is told, without a word, that it is not welcome. That is where he stopped. At the stones. While the whole village stood three rows deep around a new-raised tree and a name was being unmade, a man came up out of the lowland mist on a tired horse and reined in at the edge of everything we were, and waited to be seen.

I should not have seen him at all. My place was in the rows, my eyes down, my grief folded or pretending to be. But I had spent my whole short life learning to watch the edges of things while seeming to watch the center, and some animal part of me caught the wrongness at the boundary — a shape that did not belong to the grey morning — and I turned my head the bare width the Quiet would forgive, and I looked.

I will tell you his name, because I am the only one left who can, and because there is no longer any reason on this earth to keep it. His name was Corwin. I did not know it then. I would not know it for a long time, and when I learned it I learned it the way you learn the name of a country you have already been living in. But I have told this song too many times in too many halls where I had to call him *the stranger* and *the traveler* and *the man at the boundary*, hiding his name the way my people hid their dead, and I am done with that. He was Corwin. He was the most important thing that would ever walk up out of the mist toward me, and on the morning I first saw him I did not even know we were looking at each other.

He spoke to the Thornwatch at the stones. I was too far to hear it, but I have heard it since, from his own mouth, in the slow giving-back of years, and I can set it down as it was. He asked them — courteous, weary, in the trade-tongue of the lowlands, a man who had asked the same question at a hundred boundaries and been turned from most of them — whether anyone within still kept the old songs. Not music. He was careful to say it was not music he meant. The old songs, the true ones, the kind that hold a thing against forgetting. He was looking, he said, for someone who still remembered how to remember.

He could not have said a worse thing at a worse gate. He had ridden out of the mist into the one place in all the world that had made forgetting into law, and he had asked it, on the morning of a Severing, with a fresh name being buried not forty paces behind the stones, whether anyone here still knew how to keep the dead alive in the mouth. The Thornwatch turned him away. Of course they turned him away. They did it as we did everything, without heat, without a raised voice, a hand lifted flat that meant *no*, and *go*, and *there is nothing here for what you are asking* — and they were right, and they were wrong, and the one thing in all the wood that was exactly what he had come seeking was standing forty paces away in the third row with her dead brother's name behind her teeth, watching.

He gathered his reins to go. And before he turned the horse, he looked once, the long slow look of a man taking the measure of a place that has refused him, his eyes moving across the rows of still grey elven faces — and they crossed mine, and they stopped.

I cannot tell you what passed in that look, because nothing passed in it that either of us could have named. He did not know me. I did not know him. There was no recognition in it, not the way the songs mean recognition, no bolt, no knowing. It was smaller and stranger than that. It was the feeling of a word on the tip of the tongue — that maddening nearness of a thing you cannot reach — except that the word was a person, and the person was a stranger, and we held it between us across forty paces of cold air for the space of one breath, two, this human and this elf-girl who had never met and never would have met if the world were kind, each looking at the other and feeling, faintly, absurdly, *I have lost something that looks like you* — and then he turned the horse, and rode back down into the mist, and was gone.

I have had ages to wonder what he saw. He told me, once, near the end of my life, when I asked him — and he was quiet a long time, the way he went quiet over the things that cost him — and then he said he had not seen me at all. He had seen a girl in a crowd of the silent who was not silent. Who was holding something the way he held something. He said it was the first time in longer than he could measure that he had looked at a stranger and not been a stranger back, and he had not understood it, and it had frightened him, and he had ridden away faster than he meant to. That is all. Two people who had lost the same shape of thing, recognizing the wound and not the face. It would be years before we understood we had met. We never did fully understand it. Some songs you only get to keep, not to explain.

---

He was gone before the rite was finished, and I went home in the rows with everyone else, and I told myself it was nothing, a traveler refused, a thing that happened at the boundary and was forgotten by midday. I told myself this with great firmness all the way down the terraces. It is how I knew it was not nothing. We do not argue so hard with the things that are truly small.

I could not put his face down. That was the trouble. I, who had made a whole secret life out of keeping faces, found that I had kept his without meaning to, the way you keep a splinter — there, and there, and refusing to be ignored. And it was not the face, in the end. It was the question. *Whether anyone still kept the old songs.* He had ridden out of the whole wide loud world and come to my people's gate and asked, aloud, in daylight, for the exact forbidden thing that I crept out in the dark to do against my brother's tree, and he had asked it as though it were an ordinary thing, a thing one might simply *want*, the way you want bread or a road or a fire — and the asking had cracked something in me that the keeper-school had spent my whole life sealing shut.

Somewhere out there, the question said, this was not a crime. Somewhere out there, there were people who would have answered him.

I asked Niamh about him, careless, that evening, the way you slip a real thing inside a small one. Had she seen the traveler at the stones. Did she know what he wanted. Niamh gave me a look I knew — the fear that was the shape love took in her — and said, low, that there had been no traveler, that I should not have been watching the boundary during a Severing, that I should not have been watching anything at all. *There was no outsider*, she said, and it was not a lie so much as a thing she had decided to make true by saying it, the way our whole people made things true. I let it go. I did not let it go.

I went, two days after, to the boundary myself, in the grey of the early watch, and I asked the Thornwatch at the stones, plainly, who the man had been and what he had asked for. And the elder of them, a woman with a face like a closed door, looked at me for a long moment, and then she looked back out at the empty mist where the lowland road came up and stopped, and she said, "No one comes to the stones. Go home, child."

No one comes to the stones. He had been there. I had seen him. Forty paces, a tired horse, a question that could have got him killed in a harder age. And already, two days on, my own people had folded him away into the place they did not have to open, had unspoken him as smoothly as they unspoke their own dead, until there had been no traveler, no question, no boundary at all — only the wood, and the Quiet, and a girl who watched too much being told, gently, to go home.

I went home. And I filed him away too, in the end, though not the way they meant. I told myself I would never see him again — a stranger glimpsed once across a forbidden morning, ridden off into a world I was forbidden to follow. I was certain of it. I was certain of it the way I was certain of so many things in those last quiet days, with the whole of my real life standing just outside the boundary, waiting, while I told myself it had already gone.

I was wrong, of course. I have had a long time to be gentle with the girl who was so sure. She did not know that the man at the stones was the road her life would walk for the rest of its length. She only knew that someone had come asking for the thing she loved, and been turned away, and that she had not been able to answer him — and that this, more than the lantern in the grove, more than her mother's stilled hands, more than anything yet, had made the silence feel less like a home and more like a held breath she could not hold much longer.
