# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Chapter Seven — The Trail

---

The waystation was called Haldren's Ford, and it was the first place I had ever been that was built entirely of things that had been brought there. That is the thing about the frontier that the Nullwood had not prepared me for — everything in the wood had grown where it stood, the houses in the roots, the walkways in the branches, the lamps in the foxfire, and the whole of our world had been a world that had been *given*, shaped by patience and the long asking of the trees. Haldren's Ford was not given. It was *made* — timber hauled and stacked, stone carried and mortared, a roof of thatch that someone had cut and bound and laid by hand, and the whole of it sat on the bank of a shallow river with the look of a thing that had been built because someone had decided to build it, not because the world had offered room.

I came to it on the third day after the ditch, footsore and hungry and in the particular state of alertness that comes when you have been hunted once and know you will be hunted again. The waystation was a long low building with a common room and a fire and a woman behind the counter who looked at me the way the crossroads man had looked — with the particular attention the frontier gives to anything out of place — and then she looked at my empty hands and my muddied clothes and my elven face, and she said, "You've got nothing to pay with."

It was not a question. I said no. I had nothing. The pack was in the dark somewhere behind me, the bread was gone, the few things I had carried out of the Nullwood were scattered in the brush where I had dropped them running, and I stood in her doorway with nothing but the clothes on my back and the name of a brother no one here had ever heard.

"Tale-debt," she said, and she nodded at the common room behind her, where a dozen travelers sat at rough tables with their bowls and their cups and the particular ease of people who have been on the road a long time and have learned to be comfortable in strange rooms. "You've got a story. Everyone who walks in off the lowland road has a story. Tell it, and I'll feed you, and you can sleep by the fire, and in the morning you walk on and we never saw each other. That's the economy out here. Coin if you've got it. Story if you don't."

I had never heard of tale-debt. I would learn it, in the weeks that followed, as the one constant of the frontier — the currency of the road, the thing that passed between travelers the way coin passed in the holds and the cities, a story told for a meal, a story told for a bed, a story told for passage on a cart or a place by a fire. It was the oldest economy in the world, older than coin, older than trade, and it was the one economy the Nullwood had no equivalent for, because the Nullwood had no strangers, and the Nullwood had no road, and the Nullwood had no need of stories told to people you would never see again. I stood in the doorway of Haldren's Ford and I understood, in a way I had not understood before, that the world outside the Quiet was not a world without keeping. It was a world that kept differently — not in the wood, not in the hollow, but in the mouth, in the telling, in the passing of a story from one stranger to another across a table in a room full of smoke and noise and the smell of stew.

I told her a story. Not the true one — I was not yet ready to tell the true one, and the true one would have got me killed in a harder place than this — but a small one, a safe one, a story about a girl in a wood who had lost her way and come out the other side, and the woman listened, and the travelers listened, and when I was done the woman set a bowl in front of me and a cup and a place by the fire, and I ate, and I was warm, and I was, for the first time since the stones, among people who did not care what I had been or what I had done, and the not-caring was a kindness I had not known the world could offer.

---

It was at Haldren's Ford that I heard him spoken of for the first time since the boundary.

I was sitting by the fire with my bowl, and the common room was loud — the frontier is always loud, the way the Nullwood is always quiet, and I was learning to hold myself inside the noise the way I had learned to hold myself inside the silence — when two men at the next table began to talk of a traveler. Not by name. The frontier does not use names the way the Nullwood uses them; it uses descriptions, the way you describe a thing you have seen once and do not expect to see again. A man. Human. Alone. Passed through three days ago, maybe four, heading south toward the settlements. Asked questions — the same questions, always, the questions that marked him as strange in a world that had no patience for strangeness unless the strangeness paid.

*Whether anyone still kept the old songs.*

I set down my bowl. I did not mean to. My hands went still the way my mother's hands went still, and I sat very quiet, and I listened.

They had not thought much of him, the two men. A wanderer. A fool, probably, the kind of fool who rides the frontier asking after songs when the frontier has no time for songs and less for the men who seek them. But they remembered him. That was the thing — they *remembered* him, the way you remember a stone in a field that is not quite the color of the other stones, not remarkable enough to pick up but strange enough to notice, and they spoke of him with the particular half-dismissive attention that is the frontier's way of holding a thing it does not understand.

He had a horse. A tired one. He had paid in coin — real coin, the kind the frontier respects — and he had eaten alone, and he had asked his questions, and he had gone south, toward Thistlebrook, which was the next settlement down the road, a day's ride or two days' walk, and the men at the table moved on to other things, and I sat by the fire with my bowl cooling in my hands and my heart going hard against my ribs, and I knew.

I knew it was him. The stranger at the stones. The man who had looked at me across forty paces of cold air and seen a girl who was not silent. He had passed through this room, this very room, three days before me, and he had asked the same question he had asked at the boundary, and he had gone south, and I was south of the Nullwood with nothing and no one and a road that went in the same direction he had gone, and the world was very large and very loud and very full of people who would not care about a girl following a man she had never spoken to, and I thought: *I could find him.*

---

I want to be honest about why I followed him, because the songs we tell of our choices are forever making them nobler than they were. I did not follow him because I believed he held the key to the old songs, though some part of me hoped it. I did not follow him because I had seen something in his face at the boundary that called to me across the years, though some part of me had. I followed him because I was alone, and the world was large, and the world had teeth, and he was the only person in it who had ever asked for the thing I had nearly died for, and the thought of walking south into a country I did not know with no one and nothing and the reavers behind me and the Quiet behind them was more than I could bear, and the thought of walking south toward a person — any person, even a stranger, even a man I had never spoken to — was less than I could bear, and the difference between the two was the whole of my reason, and I will not pretend it was more than that.

It was the songs, I told myself. It was the question. With great firmness, all the way down the road to Thistlebrook, I insisted that I was following him because he sought the old songs and I was a keeper and the keeping was the thing, and the man was only the road to the keeping, and the road was only the means, and the means was only the thing a girl tells herself when she is walking toward a person because she cannot bear to walk toward nothing.

It was a lie. It was the kindest lie I had told since the keeper-school, and I have told many kind lies since, and none of them was kinder than that one. I was following him because I was alone and he was not, and because the world was loud and he had looked at me across forty paces and seen a girl who was not silent, and because the seeing was the first time in my life that a stranger had looked at me and seen the thing I was, and I wanted — I *wanted* — to be seen again.

That was the whole of it. A girl who had been seen once, walking toward the man who had seen her, and telling herself it was about the songs.

---

Thistlebrook was smaller than Haldren's Ford and louder, which I had not thought possible. It was a frontier settlement in the way that a wound is a frontier — a place where the cultivated country ended and the wild began, and the settlement sat at the edge of the ending with the look of a thing that had been built too fast and too close to the edge and was waiting, patiently, for the wild to take it back. There were horses. There were carts. There were people shouting at each other across a muddy square in a language I did not know, and the whole of it smelled of animals and woodsmoke and something frying, and I walked into it with my elven face and my empty hands and the particular alertness of a girl who has been hunted once and will not be hunted again if she can help it, and I asked.

I asked at the inn. I asked at the livery. I asked at the well, where the women gathered in the evening the way the elven women gathered at the cold hearth, except that these women did not gather in silence — they gathered in noise, in laughter, in the loud easy talk of people who have no reason to fold, and I asked them about the traveler, the human man, the one with the tired horse, and they looked at me with the particular amusement the frontier reserves for an elf asking after a human, and one of them said, "The quiet one? Passed through four days ago. Didn't say much. Asked about songs." She laughed. "Songs. As if Thistlebrook has songs."

He had gone on. South again, always south, toward a place called Karrendeep, which was a dwarven hold — a daughter-hold, small, distant, the kind of place the frontier sends its ore and its trouble — and the woman at the well told me it was a fortnight's walk and the road was bad and the reavers were worse and I should not go alone, and I thanked her, and I walked on, and behind me the women at the well laughed at something I did not hear, and I was south of Thistlebrook before the sun was down, and the road was bad, and the road went on, and I went on with it.

I was almost on him. I could feel it the way you feel a thing in the body before the mind has named it — the nearness of a person you have never spoken to, the pull of a face you have seen once across forty paces, the certainty — absurd, tender, entirely without reason — that if you walk fast enough and far enough and do not stop, you will come to the place where he is, and he will be there, and he will look at you the way he looked at you at the stones, and the looking will be the thing you have been walking toward all along.

I walked. The road went south. And somewhere ahead of me, on the same road, in the same loud world, a man who did not know he was being followed was riding toward a dwarven hold and a woman he had not yet met and a grief that would break him open in a way he could not have imagined, and I was walking toward him, and I did not know any of it, and the not-knowing was the mercy, and I walked on.
