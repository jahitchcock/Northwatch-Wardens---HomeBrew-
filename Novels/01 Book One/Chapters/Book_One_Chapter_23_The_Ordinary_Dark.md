# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Chapter Twenty-Three — The Ordinary Dark

---

She woke clear-eyed.

I had been watching for it — the shift, the surfacing, the moment the fever released its grip enough to let her through. Three days of watching. Three days of learning the rhythm of her drifting: the long silences, the murmurs, the fits of half-awareness that came and went like tide.

But this was different. Her eyes found mine and held.

"Elowen."

"Yes."

"I have been out a long time."

I did not answer. She read it in my face and did not press.

---

She asked for water. Maelis brought it — he had been keeping the waterskin cool in the stream, replacing it every hour without being asked. She drank. Her hands shook but they were her hands. Her voice was thin but it was her voice.

"Corwin is here."

"Here." He had not moved from her side in two days. He sat on the ground beside the bedroll, his back against a stone, his eyes on her face.

"Brannoc is here too."

"Getting wood, human." Brannoc's voice came from the tree line. "Couldn't sleep. Figured I'd make myself useful."

"Come here. All of you."

We came.

---

She looked at each of us. The fire was low. The dark pressed in from the trees the way it always did in the wild miles — close, patient, indifferent.

"Brannoc."

"Aye, elf."

"You are going back to your people."

He did not answer. His jaw worked.

"You are," she said. "You have been homesick since Karrendeep. I can hear it in your voice when you talk about stone. Go home. Tell them what you found out here. Tell them the world is bigger than the hold."

Brannoc swallowed. "You're telling me what to do."

"I am telling you what you already know."

He sat down heavily on a log. He did not argue.

---

"Maelis."

He stepped forward. He had been standing at the edge of the firelight, the way he always stood — close enough to guard, far enough to give space.

"You loved me."

He did not deny it. He did not speak.

"I know," she said. "I've always known. I'm sorry I couldn't love you back the way you deserved. You deserved someone who could give you all of it. I had too many roads in me."

Maelis said nothing for a long time. Then: "You gave me enough."

"That's not true."

"It is."

She smiled. It was the smile I would remember — the one that said *I know you're lying and I love you for it.*

---

"Corwin."

He leaned forward. His hand found hers.

"You found me."

"I did."

"And the song?"

"It found you first."

He closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were bright with something I had never seen in him before. Not grief — he had grieved a thousand times. This was something older. Something that had been waiting.

"I do not know how to stop," he said.

"I know."

"I have been walking for so long."

"I know."

She squeezed his hand. "Then rest. Not forever. Just now. Just this."

He bowed his head.

---

"Elowen."

"Yes."

"You're going to remember all of this."

"Yes."

"Good." She paused. Her voice was getting thinner. The clarity was a window, and the window was closing. "Remember the good parts. The laughing. The road. The way Brannoc snored."

"Hey—"

"Remember the way Corwin hummed when he thought no one was listening."

Corwin said nothing.

"Remember Maelis bringing water. Every hour. Without being asked."

Maelis looked at the ground.

"Remember that we were happy. For a season. That's what matters."

I nodded. I could not speak.

---

She slept.

Not the fever-sleep — the other kind. The kind that comes when the body has made its decision and the mind accepts it. She slept the way a child sleeps after a long day — deeply, without resistance, as though the dark were a blanket and not an ending.

We sat around her. No one spoke.

Brannoc put another log on the fire. Maelis checked her breathing. Corwin held her hand.

I kept the night.

---

She died before dawn.

There was no moment. That is what the songs get wrong — they give you a moment, a last breath, a hand loosening, a word unfinished. But death does not announce itself. It does not pause for witnesses. It simply arrives in the space between one breath and the next, and when you look for the breath that should come, it is not there.

I was watching. I saw her chest rise. I waited for it to fall. It did not.

I waited longer.

It did not.

---

Maelis knew first. He had watched too many die. He moved to her side, placed two fingers at her wrist, and closed his eyes.

"She has crossed."

Brannoc made a sound — low, dwarven, the kind of sound the stone makes when it splits along a fault line. He turned away and walked into the trees.

Corwin did not move. His hand was still holding hers. His face was still. Too still. The kind of stillness that comes not from calm but from a grief so practiced it has learned to arrive without disturbing the furniture.

I sat beside him. I did not touch him. I did not speak.

*"I did not yet know that I would spend the rest of my life learning how to sit beside the grieving without trying to fix what could not be fixed."*

---

The terrible practical stillness.

Someone had to tend the body. Someone had to break the camp. Someone had to decide which direction to walk, and when, and whether to bury her here or carry her to consecrated ground.

Maelis took the practical work. He had done this before — the washing, the laying-out, the folding of hands. He moved with the quiet efficiency of a man performing a rite he had performed a hundred times. His hands were steady. His face was not.

Brannoc came back from the trees with his eyes red and his voice rough. Without being asked, he began to break the camp — packing the bedrolls, dousing the fire, working the way dwarves work when the grief is too large for words. His hands moved through stone and leather and rope, through the small repeated motions that keep a man from falling apart.

Corwin sat beside the body. He had not let go of her hand.

I watched him. I watched the grief move through him — not the fresh grief of a man who has just lost someone he loved, but the rehearsed grief of a man who has stood at this exact bedside a hundred times and knows every step of the dance.

He knew what came next. He knew the shape of the hours, the weight of the silence, the way the world would go on being ordinary while everything inside him was not.

He knew it too well.

And that frightened me more than the grief itself.
