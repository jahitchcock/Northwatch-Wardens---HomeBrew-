# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Chapter Thirteen — Small Impossibilities

---

I started keeping him without meaning to. The songs we tell of our loves are forever making them into things we chose, and I did not choose this. I did not choose to keep Corwin. I did not choose to gather the small impossibilities that did not add up, that made a picture I could not yet see. I simply did what the Nullwood had taught me, what the hollow had trained me, what the gift had made me do — I kept. I gathered. I held.

But I am ahead of myself. I am always ahead of myself, in the telling. That is the curse of the Memory-Keeper — you know the end before you have finished singing the beginning.

---

The first one was the wound.

We were three days south of Karrendeep, camped in a hollow between two hills. Corwin was tending his side — the place where the deep-beast's claw had caught him in the mine. I was watching. I had learned to see the things that were not meant to be seen.

The wound was gone.

Not healing. Not scarring. Not closing the way wounds close, slowly, patiently. Gone. As though it had never been there.

I said, "Where."

"Where is what."

"The wound. The claw. The thing that caught you in the mine."

He looked down at his side. "It healed."

"It didn't heal. It's gone. Like it was never there."

"I have always healed swiftly. Some do."

"Not _that_ fast."

He looked at me. The door behind his eyes was shut. "I have always healed swiftly. It is what makes me useful in a fight and useless in a hospital."

He said it with the deflection he used when speaking about things he did not want to speak about. The deflection was a thing I had learned to recognize. I let it go. I filed it. The first impossibility.

But that is the rest of the song. This was still the beginning. Still the first small impossibility, the noticing, the watching, the keeping.

---

The second one was the language.

We were a week south of Karrendeep, camped in a ruin — old, older than anything the road passed now. Corwin was asleep. I was awake. The Nullwood had taught me to sleep lightly, and the light sleeping let me hear the things that were not meant to be heard.

He was speaking.

Not in the trade-tongue. Not in the language of the Nullwood. Not in the language of the dwarves. In a language I had never heard — old, older than anything I had a word for. The kind of old that makes the skin prickle. A language that should not exist.

He was speaking in his sleep in a tongue that was dead, older than dead, the kind of dead that makes the stone forget.

In the morning, I asked him. "What."

"I was not speaking."

"You were. In your sleep. A language I've never heard. Old. Older than anything I know."

He looked at the fire. "I dream in old languages. It signifies nothing. Do not concern yourself."

He said it with the same deflection. I let it go. I filed it. The second impossibility.

---

The third one was the road.

We were two weeks south of Karrendeep, walking through country that was flat and open, the kind of empty that makes the sky enormous and the person small. Corwin was looking at the road in a way I had learned to recognize.

"I remember when this road was not here," he said.

I stopped. "What."

"This road. I remember when it was not here. When this was a field. When this was grass. When it had not been walked yet."

"How could you remember that."

He blinked. The door behind his eyes opened a crack and closed again. "I cannot say. I simply do. Let the matter fall."

The deflection was smooth. I let it go. I filed it. The third impossibility.

---

There were more. There were always more. The way he knew the names of stars that had not been named in the trade-tongue for centuries. The way he could read the weather with a certainty that was not learned but remembered. The way he would stop, in the middle of a sentence, and look at a tree or a hill or a bend in the road with the look of a man seeing something he had seen before — many times, so many times that the seeing had become a kind of grief.

I kept them all. I gathered them. I held them. A private list in the place where I kept the small things that did not add up. The things that would one day, when there were enough of them, make a picture.

I did not yet know what the picture was a picture of.

---

It was on a night, three weeks south of Karrendeep, that I broke the bargain.

We were camped in a wood — not the Nullwood, not the kind of wood that absorbs sound, but a normal wood, a loud wood, full of the sounds of animals and wind and the things that move in the dark. The loudness was a thing I had learned to love. It made me bold.

I said, "Who."

He looked at me. The fire was between us. "What."

"Who. You are grieving someone. I can see it. I can hear it. I can feel it." I paused. "I know asking breaks our bargain. But I need to know."

The door behind his eyes opened — not the crack, not the narrow space, but the whole door. The whole opening. The thing he had been keeping shut, now for the first time letting open. It frightened him. I saw it.

But he was not ready. Not yet. Not that night.

"I am grieving everyone," he said. "Everyone I have ever loved. Everyone I have ever lost. Everyone I have ever known and cared about and held and let go. Everyone I have failed to save, failed to keep, failed to be enough for." He looked at the fire. "That is what I carry. That is what makes me what I am. And the what-I-am is the thing you are walking with. You can unchoose that at any time. I will understand."

The deflection was less smooth this time. Less practiced. I noticed.

But that is the rest of the song. This was still the asking, and the asking was what broke the bargain, and the breaking made us something more than companions.

We sat by the fire. We did not speak. The silence between us was not the silence of the Nullwood, not the silence of the road, not the silence of the first day. A fifth kind of silence. The silence of two people who have asked and been asked and are not yet ready to answer, walking toward the answering the way you walk toward a thing you know is coming and are not yet ready for. The not-yet-ready-ness is the mercy. The mercy lets the walking go on.
