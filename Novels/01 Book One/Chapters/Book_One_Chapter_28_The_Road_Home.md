# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Chapter Twenty-Eight — The Road Home

---

We walked north.

Not together — not exactly. We walked the same road in the same direction, but we were already separating. The separation had begun the night before, in the silence that was not the Quiet, in the recognition that had locked the bond into its final shape. We were two people who had survived something together and were now choosing different paths forward.

I was going home. To the Nullwood. To the Quiet and the elders and the house we had built around our silence.

Corwin was going — I did not know where. He had not said. He had only turned his back on the Hollow Choir and walked north, and north was the direction I was walking too, and so we walked.

---

Three weeks on the road.

The wild miles gave way to the Thinning, and the Thinning gave way to the first green edges of the Nullwood's reach. The trees thickened. The air changed. The light filtered through canopy the way it always did in the deep forest — dappled, shifting, alive with the movement of leaves and the sound of things growing.

We spoke little. Not from silence — from understanding. The things we needed to say had been said, or had been left deliberately unsaid, and the space between us was comfortable in the way that only the space between two people who truly see each other can be comfortable.

On the second week, he hummed.

Not the old song — not the one Wren had known, the one from before. A different tune. Something smaller, something personal, something that belonged to him and not to the ages. He hummed it the way you hum when you are walking and the walking has its own rhythm and the rhythm wants a companion.

I listened. I kept the melody. I did not tell him I was keeping it.

---

On the last day, we reached the fork.

The left road led into the Nullwood — into the deep forest, into the Quiet, into the home I had left and the home I would now change. The right road led east, toward the frontier settlements, toward the road that had no end and the walking that had no destination.

We stopped.

"This is where we part," Corwin said.

"Yes."

He looked at me. His face was composed — the ancient composure, the one that had survived everything. But his eyes were not composed. His eyes held something I had not seen in them before. Not grief. Not wonder. Something quieter than either.

Gratitude.

"You kept her," he said.

"I spoke her."

"That is the same thing."

I shook my head. "It is not. Keeping is what the Quiet does. Speaking is what I do. They are opposites."

He considered this. "Perhaps."

---

He reached into his coat and brought out something small. A stone — no, not a stone. A piece of carved bone, old and smooth, worn by centuries of handling. It was pale, almost white, and it had a shape I could not quite name — not a rune, not a letter, not a symbol from any tradition I knew.

"Take this."

I took it. It was warm from his pocket. It was lighter than it looked.

"What is it."

"I do not know. I have carried it for longer than I can remember. It was given to me by someone I have forgotten, in an age I can no longer name. I do not know what it means. I only know that it has been with me through every road and every loss and every burial, and it has never failed me."

I closed my hand around it.

"Why give it to me."

"Because you will remember what I will forget. And the thing should be with someone who remembers."

---

I wanted to ask him to stay.

The wanting was there — clear, sharp, the kind of wanting that sits in the chest like a stone and does not move. I wanted him to walk into the Nullwood with me. I wanted him to sit beside me in the grove and listen to me speak the names and watch the Quiet crack. I wanted him to be part of the thing I was building, the practice I was founding, the song I was learning to sing.

But I did not ask.

Because asking would have been clinging. And I had learned, at Wren's grave, that clinging kills what you are trying to save. I had practiced release with the dead woman. Now I practiced it with the living man.

I let him go.

"Walk well," I said.

"And you."

He turned. He walked east, toward the road that had no end. His stride was long and even, the gait of a man who has walked across continents and knows the rhythm of distance.

I watched him until the trees took him.

Then I turned and walked into the Nullwood.

---

The forest closed around me.

The canopy thickened. The light dimmed. The air grew cool and still, the way it always did in the deep places where the Verdant Mother's presence was strongest and the old songs were loudest.

I walked alone.

Wren's life. Maelis's grief. Corwin's unspoken weight and the carved bone in my pocket and the melody he had hummed on the second day. All of it carried — not hoarded, not hidden, but spoken. Kept in the new way. Given away to the air and the trees and the silence that was waiting for me.

I was no longer the girl who had whispered her brother's name to a tree and believed a thing kept was a thing saved. I was becoming something else. Something the Nullwood had not seen before. Something the Quiet had not prepared for.

I was becoming the Keeper.

*"I did not know the word yet. I would not know it for many years, when the practice had spread and the order had taken shape and the people who came after me needed a name for what I had become. But the becoming had started here, on the road home, in the space between the parting and the arrival."*
