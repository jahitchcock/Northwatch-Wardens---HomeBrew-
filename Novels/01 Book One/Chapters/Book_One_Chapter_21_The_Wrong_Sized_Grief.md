# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Chapter Twenty-One — The Wrong-Sized Grief

---

Corwin had grieved before.

He had grieved across centuries, across wars, across the endless dying of everyone he loved. He had grieved in the First Age and the Second and the Third. He had grieved through kingdoms that rose and fell, through lovers who aged and died while he did not, through friends whose bones turned to dust and whose names were forgotten.

He had grieved so much grief that grief had become a familiar garment.

But this grief was wrong-sized.

It was too vast and too small. Too vast because Wren was everything — the living song he had chased for ages, the voice that had summoned him out of his long death. Too small because he had known her for such a short season. The grief was too large for a love this new. It did not fit.

And yet it fit worse when he tried to set it down.

---

Maelis had an old stillness in his hands. The kind that comes from watching over the dying. He made no sound when he entered, but I saw him turn, saw the recognition pass between them. Maelis and Corwin, in the same room. No rivalry now. No mistrust. Only the shared dread of what was coming.

"I have watched many die," Maelis said. It was a statement, not a comfort.

Corwin said nothing. He did not move. He held Wren's hand and watched the slow rhythm of her breathing.

"I have watched many die," Maelis said again, "and it never becomes something you can bear."

They sat together on either side of Wren. The hunter and the death-Aspected man. The rivals. The old enemies. And I watched them both hold the same grief in their hands.

---

I carried both of their griefs. Not because I was asked — because I could.

Corwin's grief was ancient and rehearsed, carved into him by centuries of loss. He knew the shape of it, the weight of it, the way it would sit in his chest. He could predict its course the way a sailor predicts weather. He had walked this road before.

But this time was different. I saw it in the set of his shoulders. The grief, and the fear.

Maelis's grief was quieter. He had held many dying hands. He had sung many souls across. But Wren was not a ritual to him, not a duty, not a passage to be performed. She was someone he had come to love.

I kept both of their griefs. I held them in my keeping and I did not set them down.

---

I moved between them. Corwin first — I sat beside him and matched his silence.

"Maelis," I said, "what do you sing for the dying?"

Neither of them looked at me. The question hung in the air. Maelis answered slowly: "The leaving song. The thing the old ones gave for those who walk across. It does not hurry them. It does not hold them. It makes the passage gentle, that is all."

"Then sing it," I said. "Not yet. But when the time comes. Sing it."

Maelis nodded. Corwin's hand tightened around Wren's.

---

The night settled. The wrong-sized grief was too large for the room and too small for what it held. We sat in it the way people sit in a storm — close, together, waiting.

I did not know how long it would take. I did not know if dawn would find her alive or gone. I only knew that I would keep this grief, and that I would not set it down.
