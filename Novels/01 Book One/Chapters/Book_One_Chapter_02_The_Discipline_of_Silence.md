# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Chapter Two — The Discipline of Silence

---

The bell for the keeper-school was not a bell. We had no bells in the Nullwood; a struck thing rings, and a ring carries, and we did not let sound carry if we could help it. What called us to our lessons each morning was a hollow length of grey wood, hung in the fork of the teaching-tree, that the eldest apprentice would strike once with a wrapped mallet — a single low note, more felt than heard, that died before it reached the second row of houses.

I have heard, since, the great bronze bells of the holds and the temples, that fling their voices for leagues and call it joy. I did not grow up with joy that loud. I grew up with a sound made so it would not be a sound, and I did not know, then, that this was strange. A child takes the shape of the world it is given the way water takes the shape of the cup. You have to be poured out before you learn you ever had a shape at all.

I rose that morning on no sleep and a fear I had folded small, and I went to be taught how to fold it smaller.

That was the first lie of the day, though no one had asked me a question yet. The lie was in the going — in walking the path to the keeper-school with my face arranged into the calm they wanted, as though I had not knelt the night before at my brother's tree and said the one word that the whole of that school existed to unsay.

---

We called them keepers, the elders who taught us. I have thought a long time about that word.

A keeper is one who keeps. A keeper of bees, of a gate, of a hold — one who holds a thing safe and whole against the wearing of the world. That is what the word had meant once, in the Nullwood, before the Withering: a keeper was one who kept the dead, who carried their names and their small true histories forward so that nothing of them was lost. To keep a thing was to speak it. That was the whole of the art.

By the time I came to the keeper-school, the word had been turned inside out, the way a current will turn a thing it carries until you cannot tell the face of it from the back. Our keepers kept nothing. They taught us, with great gentleness, how to let the dead go down into silence and stay there. They called it keeping because no one had the heart to call it what it was.

That morning, as on every morning, we sat in a ring on the smooth roots of the teaching-tree, and Keeper Ilvath taught us the fold.

I want to set him down carefully, here at the beginning, because of what comes after, and because the songs we make of our betrayers are forever turning them into something simpler than a person, and I have promised to keep him whole even though he did not keep me.

Ilvath was old, even for us. The Withering had taken his wife and both his daughters in a single season, and where another man might have broken loud, Ilvath had broken quiet, and then he had spent the long years since teaching the rest of us to break the same way, because he believed — he truly believed, I never doubted it, not even at the end — that it was the only mercy a people with our memory could bear. We do not forget, the Nullwood elves. That is the thing the short-lived races never understand about us. A human grief wears thin with the years because the years wear the human thin. Ours do not wear thin. We carry every face as fresh as the day it left us, for centuries, for as long as we last, and Ilvath had looked at that — at a whole people condemned to carry perfect, undimming grief across a span of years no human heart could imagine — and he had decided that silence was kinder than memory.

He was not wrong about the weight. I have come to think he was wrong about almost everything else. But he was not wrong about the weight, and I will not pretend, even now, that he was.

"Bring up the grief," he said to us that morning, in the soft even voice he always used. "Whichever one is nearest. Bring it up whole. Look at it."

We brought them up. Around the ring I felt the others go still and inward, each of them turning toward some private dark.

"Now," Ilvath said. "Do not speak it. Do not let it have the name. Hold the face, and fold the face — small, and smaller — until it will fit in a place you do not have to open. That is the keeping. That is the whole of the keeping."

I sat with my brother's face bright behind my eyes and I did not fold it. I sat with it open. I looked at Fenn — the unfinished laugh, the way he had of standing in a doorway as though he had all the time in the world, which, it turned out, he had not — and I held him open and unfolded in the one place they could not see, and I let my face do the calm thing it had learned to do.

Ilvath's eyes found me. They always found me; I think he watched me more than the others, though I did not understand why until much later, when I understood that he had seen in me, from the first, the thing he had spent his life trying to put out. He looked at me, and he knew. I am certain now that he knew. He saw a girl with her grief sitting open in her lap like a thing she refused to put down, and he saw the lie in my calm face, and he said nothing.

"Is it folded?" he asked me. Quietly. Only me.

"Yes, Keeper," I said.

That was the second lie, and the first one anyone heard, and he let it pass. He let it pass the way you let a child keep a secret you have already guessed, out of some tenderness that is also, if you are honest, a kind of cowardice — the not wanting to be the one who breaks the thing. He let it pass that morning, and the next, and a great many mornings, and I have wondered since whether everything would have gone differently if he had not. If he had stopped me early, in kindness, the way you stop a child before the fall instead of after. But that is not the song that happened. The song that happened is that a gentle man saw what I was and looked away, again and again, until the day came that he could not, and on that day he voted to send me into the dark, and wept while he did it.

But I am ahead of myself. He let it pass. That is all that happened that morning. He let it pass, and we folded our dead, or pretended to, and the lesson ended, and I thought I had won something.

---

After the lesson, while the others went out into the morning, I stayed to put away the teaching-things, because it was my turn and because staying gave me a reason to be near the case.

There was a case at the back of the keeper-school, a long low thing of dark wood, and in it the keepers kept — truly kept, this once — the relics of the art from before it was turned inside out. They were study-pieces. We were shown them so that we would understand what we had been saved from, the way you might show a child the swollen river so it will fear the water. I had been shown them many times. I had never once been shown them the way I saw them when I was alone.

I lifted the lid and I took out the name-cord.

You will not have seen one. There are very few left, now, even in my keeping, and they were old already when I was young. A name-cord is a length of soft cord, arm's length or longer, knotted along its whole run — and each knot is a name. The old keepers tied them as the dead came, one knot for one life, and they did not write the name anywhere, because the cord was not a list. The cord was a way of remembering to *say*. You ran it through your fingers, knot by knot, in the long evenings, and at each knot you spoke the name and as much of the life as you carried, aloud, to whoever would listen or to no one at all — and in the speaking the dead stayed. Not as a weight folded away in a place you could not open. As a voice you kept lending them, so they could go on a little longer in the only world that was left to them, which was the world of the said.

I ran the cord through my fingers in the empty hall. I did not know a single name on it. They were all of them gone past saying, the people of those knots, gone so far past saying that not even I, who would spend my whole long life saying, could call them back; their keepers had died, and the saying had stopped, and a stopped name is a name that finishes its dying. That is the thing I understood in the empty keeper-school with the cord in my hands, the thing the case was meant to teach and taught me exactly backward. They had shown me the cord to make me grateful for the silence. What I felt was not gratitude. What I felt was the most enormous grief I had yet felt in my life, grief for a hall full of strangers I would never be able to keep, and under the grief, rising, a kind of fury — that we had been given the one gift that could hold a thing against the dark, and had agreed, all of us, gently, to set it down.

"You're holding it too long."

I had not heard Niamh come back in. I never heard Niamh; she moved the way water moves, the way I have only ever seen our people move, and she was beside me with her hand already closing the lid before I had finished startling.

"You hold it too long every time," she said, low, "and one of these days it won't be me who sees." She took the cord out of my fingers — gently, gently, she was always gentle with me, which is the part I have never been able to forgive myself — and she laid it back in its place and shut the lid, and she looked at me with the particular fear that was, in her, the shape love took. "Whatever you're doing," she said, "stop doing it. Or do it where I don't have to lie about it."

"I'm not doing anything," I said.

That was the third lie. She knew it was a lie. She covered it anyway. She put her arm through mine and walked me out into the loud bright morning as though we were only two girls who had stayed to tidy the hall, and she talked of small things the whole way, to give my silence somewhere to hide, and I let her, and I loved her, and I did not once think — why would I have thought it? — that the day would come when her covering for me would be the very thing that uncovered me, and that she would carry that to the end of her days as a wound she never earned.

Keep her whole, I told myself, beginning this. Keep Niamh whole. She did nothing but love a girl who would not be careful with her.

---

I came home that evening to the house we had built around our silence, and my mother was at the cold hearth, and Fenn's cloak was on its peg, and the day's fury had not gone out of me, and I did a foolish, tender thing.

I tried to give her his name.

She was mending something by the last of the light — my mother's hands were never still; I think the stillness frightened her, because the stillness was where the folded thing waited — and I sat down near her, near enough, and I said, softly, the way you put your hand toward a wounded animal, "I was thinking of Fenn today."

I did not even say the forbidden thing. I did not say his name to her. I only said *I was thinking of him*, which was no crime, which was barely even a breaking of the Quiet, which any people in the world but ours would have called the smallest and most ordinary thing a sister could say.

My mother's hands stopped.

I have kept that stillness. Of all the things I have kept across all the years, I have kept the way my mother's hands went still over her mending in the last grey light, because in that stillness I saw the whole of what the Quiet had done to her, what it had done to all of us, what it would do to me if I let it. She did not weep. She did not turn to me. She did not say his name back, or ask what I had thought, or let the door I had opened stay open even the width of a breath. She folded. I watched her do it — watched a woman take the name of her own dead son, offered to her by her living daughter in the dark of their own house, and fold it down, small, and smaller, into the place she did not have to open — and when her hands began to move again over the mending they moved quite steadily, and she said, in a voice with nothing in it at all, "It's late. You should sleep."

That was all. *It's late. You should sleep.*

I went up to my small dark, and I lay in it as I had lain the night before, and somewhere below me my mother sat mending in the silence she had chosen, or that had been chosen for her so long ago she could no longer tell the difference, and I understood, that night, the thing that set my whole life moving.

I was looking at my future. I was looking at a hundred years of it, and two hundred, and five — at the long elven span of years stretching out in front of me, every one of them spent learning to fold a little smaller, until I was a woman whose hands stopped when her child said a name, and then started again, steady, with nothing in them at all.

I could not do it. That was all. It was not yet courage; courage came later, and cost more. That first night it was only a kind of animal refusal, the way a creature will not walk back into the trap it has already felt close on it once. I lay in the dark and I knew, with my whole body, that I could not fold myself away for five hundred years, and that one day, not yet, but one day, I was going to do something about it.

And down the hall my mother's door was closed, and behind it there was no sound, and there is no sound in all my long memory that I have found harder to keep than that one — the sound of my mother, not weeping, in a house where her son's name was not allowed.
