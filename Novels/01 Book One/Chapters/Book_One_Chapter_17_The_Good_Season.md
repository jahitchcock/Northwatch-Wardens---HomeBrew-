# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Chapter Seventeen — The Good Season

---

They fell in love over road-craft and bad jokes. The songs make love grand and tragic, but this was not grand and not tragic. It was the kind of thing that makes the heart light, the road short, the night warm. It was good. And the goodness made you forget, for a while, that it was a tragedy.

It started with a map. Wren was showing Corwin the route through the wild miles, the path to the Hollow Choir. She pointed at the map. "Here. There. This is where the water is. This is where the danger is."

Corwin looked. Then he said, "That's not right."

"What's not right."

"That river. It does not go there." He pointed — not at the map, but at the land. At what he remembered. "It goes there."

"Where the map says it doesn't."

"Yes."

"How do you know that."

He looked at her. He looked at the land. "I have been here before."

"When."

"A long time ago. Longer than you would believe. Longer than the map remembers."

She laughed. Bright and easy, the kind of laugh that makes the world feel lighter. "You are strange, human. Stranger than I thought. Stranger than the map. Stranger than the river. I like it."

---

It went on like that for weeks. The whole of the good season — the heart of the song, the part that made you forget it was a tragedy.

They fell in love over shared road-craft and shared humor and the kind of small things that make a love real and earned.

He stopped guarding himself. By inches. The small steps a man takes when he is walking toward something he wants and is afraid to want it.

He laughed — the way he had not laughed since I met him, the way he had not laughed since before whatever had made him the way he was.

He touched her. Tentatively, the way you touch something you are afraid will break. And she touched him back. Without fear.

I watched. I was happy for them. That is the truth I want to be honest about. There was no jealousy in me, no bitterness. The heart can hold more than one love, and mine held theirs without complaint.

---

It was Wren who asked me to keep something.

We were sitting by the fire, the five of us. The night was warm. She said, "Elowen. I have been thinking about your craft. About the keeping. I think it is beautiful. I want you to keep something for me."

"What."

"Not a dead thing. A living thing. A small joy. This moment. This fire. This family. The thing that is happening now, that is good, that is worth the keeping."

I looked at her. Then at Corwin. Then at the fire. "All right. I'll keep it."

And I did. I held it gently, the way you hold a thing that is precious.

Corwin watched. Then he said something quiet, something soft: "The best things should not be hoarded. They should be spent. Given away. Shared."

For one beat, his philosophy of keeping-versus-clinging sounded like wisdom instead of warning.

---

But that is the rest of the song. This was still the good season, the falling, the happening. We sat by the fire, five of us, a family. The more-than-ness of us would break our hearts before the song was done. But the breaking was still ahead.
