# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Chapter Eight — The Reluctant Stranger

---

I found him at a waystation called Greymarch, two days south of Thistlebrook, on a road that had stopped pretending to be a road and had become a track through scrubland and broken country. The waystation was a stone building with a thatched roof and a yard full of horses and the smell of woodsmoke and wet wool, and it sat at the edge of a stretch of country the frontier called the Thinning — a place where the settlements grew farther apart and the wild grew closer and the road went from bad to worse and the reavers grew bold enough to work in daylight.

He was in the yard when I came in. I knew him before I saw his face — I knew the horse, the same tired horse from the descriptions, a grey with a white blaze and a way of standing that said it had been ridden a long time and would be ridden a long time more — and then I saw him, and I stopped, and I stood in the yard with my boots full of road and my heart full of something I had not expected, which was the shock of a face you have carried in your memory for weeks turning out to be a real face, a face that belongs to a person who is standing twenty paces away and does not know you are there.

He was not what I had expected. I do not know what I had expected — someone larger, perhaps, someone who filled the space the way the stranger at the stones had filled the space, the way a question fills a room. He was not large. He was a man of middle height, lean in the way of people who have walked a long time and eaten when they could, and his face was the face I had kept from the boundary — the face that had looked at me across forty paces and seen a girl who was not silent — except that now, up close, I could see the things the distance had smoothed over. The lines at the corners of his eyes that said he had squinted into more sunrises than I could count. The set of his mouth that said he had said goodbye more times than he had said hello. The hands — I noticed the hands — that were scarred in the way of a man who has used them for things he does not talk about, and who uses them still, and who will go on using them until they stop working, which, for this man, I would learn, was a very long time.

He was tacking his horse. He had not seen me. I stood in the yard and I watched him work, and I felt the particular terror of a girl who has walked a hundred miles toward a stranger and now has to do the thing she has not prepared for, which is speak to him.

---

"You're the one who was at the stones."

I said it before I could stop myself. The words came out of me the way a name comes out of the hollow — not planned, not careful, just *there*, spoken aloud in the yard of a waystation in a country I did not know, to a man who was tacking his horse and had not expected to be addressed by an elf-girl with mud on her boots and nothing in her hands.

He stopped. He did not turn. He stood with his hands on the saddle and his back to me, and I watched his shoulders go still the way my shoulders went still in the grove when the lantern came, and then he turned, slowly, and he looked at me.

He looked at me the way he had looked at me at the boundary — the long slow look, the taking of measure — except that now there was no distance between us, no forty paces, no rows of silent elven faces, only the yard and the horse and the grey morning and a girl who had walked out of the Nullwood and across the frontier and into his path, and he looked at me and I saw the recognition land, not as a bolt, not as a knowing, but as the same small strange feeling I had felt at the stones — the feeling of a word on the tip of the tongue, the nearness of a thing you cannot reach.

"You are a long way from your wood," he said.

His voice was not what I had expected either. I had expected — I do not know what I had expected. Something larger, something that matched the weight of the question he had asked at the stones. His voice was quiet. Dry. Warm in the way that a fire is warm when you are close enough to feel it but not close enough to sit by it — a warmth held at arm's length, offered and withdrawn in the same breath.

"I was exiled," I said.

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he went back to tacking his horse, and he said, over his shoulder, in the same dry quiet voice, "You should go back."

"I can't."

"Then you should go somewhere else." He tightened a strap. "This road goes south. South is not a good place for someone who has just been exiled from a wood that does not let sound out." He paused. "You are easy to read. You walk like someone who has never had to run. You eat like someone who has never had to go without. And you are standing in a waystation yard in the Thinning with nothing on you and no one with you, and the reavers work this stretch in daylight now." He looked at me again, and this time the warmth was closer, the fire a little less at arm's length, and he said, "Go to the next settlement. Stay where there are people. Do not walk the road alone."

"I'm not going to the next settlement."

"Then where are you going."

It was not a question. It was a statement wearing the shape of a question, the way the Quiet wore the shape of mercy, and I heard it for what it was — a man who has been asked too many questions by too many people and has learned to answer them by asking them back, harder, until the asker goes away.

"I'm going where you're going," I said.

He stopped tacking the horse. He stood very still, and then he turned to face me fully, and I saw his face do a thing I would see it do many times in the years that followed — a closing, a shutting, a door pulled shut behind the eyes — and he said, "No."

---

I should have left. I know that now, with the years between us, and I knew it then, in the yard, in the grey morning, with his *no* hanging in the air between us like a thing set down and not picked up. I should have taken his advice and gone to the next settlement and stayed where there were people and let the man who had asked for the old songs ride south alone into the country that was not good, because the country was not good and I was not ready for it and he had told me so, plainly, in the dry warm voice of a man who has said goodbye more times than he has said hello and has learned to say it early, before the leaving costs more than it should.

I did not leave. I sat down on a barrel in the yard and I waited, because I had walked a hundred miles to find him and I was not going to walk away because he said no, and the saying-no was the thing I had expected, and the waiting was the thing I had prepared for, and I sat on the barrel and I watched him work, and he watched me watching, and neither of us said anything, and the horse stood between us with the patient indifference of an animal that has seen this before.

He finished tacking. He checked his pack. He did not look at me. And then, because he was Corwin and because Corwin could not, even then, leave a thing alone once it had been set in front of him, he said, "Why."

I had been waiting for that. I had been walking toward it since Haldren's Ford, since the well at Thistlebrook, since the ditch, since the stones, and I had not prepared an answer because the answer was not a thing you prepare, it was a thing you *say*, and I said it.

"You asked at the stones whether anyone still kept the old songs. I do. I was exiled for it. And I have walked a hundred miles to find you because you are the only person I have ever met who asked for the thing I was exiled for, and I want to know why you asked it, and I want to know what you are looking for, and I want —" I stopped. I had not meant to say the last part. I said it anyway. "I want to not be alone."

He looked at me. The door behind his eyes was still shut, but something had moved in the room behind it, something I could not see but could feel, the way you feel a person moving in a dark room you are standing outside of. He looked at me for a long time, and then he looked away, and then he looked back, and he said, "I am not good company."

"I know."

"I do not explain myself."

"I know."

"I do not talk about where I have been or where I am going or why."

"I know."

He was quiet. The yard was quiet. The horse shifted its weight and the leather creaked and somewhere inside the waystation someone was laughing, the loud easy laugh of the frontier, and Corwin stood in the yard with his hand on the saddle and his face doing the thing I would learn to read — the closing and the opening, the door shutting and the crack appearing in the same breath — and he said, "You are going to follow me whether I say yes or no."

"Yes."

He let out a breath. Not a sigh. Not a laugh. Something smaller, something that was the sound of a man who has been alone a very long time and has just been offered company and does not know what to do with the offering because the being-alone has become so much a part of him that the not-being-alone feels like a thing he will break if he holds it.

"Then ride," he said. "Or walk. I am not giving you the horse."

---

We did not speak for the first day. That was his condition, though he did not call it a condition and I did not call it anything; it was simply the shape of the thing, a man and a girl on a road going south, the man on the horse and the girl walking beside it, and the silence between them was not the silence of the Nullwood and not the silence of the road, but a third kind of silence, the silence of two people who have agreed to be near each other and have not yet agreed to be known.

I watched him. I could not help it. I watched the way he sat the horse, easy, as though the riding were not a thing he did but a thing he had always done, the way breathing is a thing you have always done, and I watched the way his eyes moved across the country, not the way my eyes moved — not the watchful stillness of the grove, but something else, something older, the way a man looks at a landscape he has crossed too many times to count and has learned to read the way you read a page, quickly, for the things that matter, the water, the shelter, the places where trouble might hide.

And I watched his hands. The scarred hands. The hands that tacked the horse and checked the pack and held the reins with the ease of long use, and that, when he thought I was not looking, did a thing I had never seen a man's hands do — they moved, faintly, in the air beside the saddle, the fingers opening and closing as though they were holding something that was not there, and then they stopped, and he was still again, and I looked away, and I filed it, and I did not understand it, and I would not understand it for a long time.

It was near evening when the crack appeared. We had stopped at a stream to water the horse, and he was kneeling at the bank, and I was sitting on a rock with my boots off and my feet in the cold water, and the silence between us had softened the way silence softens when it has been held long enough, and I said, without meaning to, without planning it, the way you say a thing that has been sitting behind your teeth and finds its way out when the teeth are tired:

"You've lost someone."

He went still. Not the stillness of the grove. A different stillness — deeper, older, the stillness of a man who has been asked a question he has answered too many times and has learned to answer by going still, because the stillness is the only answer that does not cost more than he can pay.

"That is not a question," he said.

"No."

He was quiet for a long time. The stream ran over the stones. The horse drank. And then he said, in the dry warm voice, the voice that held the warmth at arm's length and offered it and withdrew it in the same breath, "Everyone I have ever known."

I did not say anything. I did not need to. The keeping was not in the words; it was in the listening, in the way I sat on the rock with my feet in the water and my face open and my silence not folded but held, held the way the old keepers held a name, with the whole of the attention, with the whole of the care, and he looked at me and I saw the crack — the small, unwilling, entirely human crack in the door behind his eyes — and I saw him feel it, and I saw him close it, and I saw him stand up from the stream and walk back to the horse and put his hand on the saddle and say, "We should move. This stretch is not safe after dark."

He said no again, after that. Not in words. In the set of his shoulders, in the way he rode a little ahead of me for the next mile, in the particular distance he kept between us, the distance that said *I have let you near me and I am sorry for it and I will not let you near me again.* I understood it. I had done the same thing, in the grove, with the lantern and the bark and the name I had said and then not said again. The crack frightens you more than the wall. The wall you know. The crack is the thing you did not expect, and the not-expecting is the thing that makes you put the wall back up, harder, until the next crack comes, and it always comes.

---

The waystation at Greymarch was where we stopped for the night, and it was where the reavers came.

I have told this part of the song in many halls, and I have always told it carefully, because it is the first time in the song that blood is drawn, and the first time is the time you must get right, because the first time tells the listener what kind of song this is — whether it is a song that flinches or a song that does not, whether it is a song that makes its violence beautiful or a song that makes its violence true. I have tried always to make it true. I will try now.

It was after dark. The common room was full — travelers, teamsters, a family heading south to the settlements, a pair of dwarven prospectors with the look of men who had been in the hills too long and were glad of the fire and the noise — and Corwin and I were at a table near the back, and we had not spoken since the stream, and the silence between us had hardened again, and I was eating and he was not, and the night was the night, and the fire was the fire, and the world was the world, and then the world changed.

The door came in. Not opened — *came in*, the whole door, the wood and the hinges and the frame, and behind it the dark and the noise and the men, and the first thing I heard was not a shout but a sound I had never heard before and would hear again, the sound of a waystation being taken — the crash, the scream, the overturning of a table, the particular pitch of a room full of people who have been surprised in the dark and do not yet know what is happening and will not know until it is too late.

Reavers. A dozen of them. Maybe more. They came through the door and the windows and the yard, and they came with the efficiency of men who have done this before and know the shape of a waystation the way a butcher knows the shape of a carcass, and they came with blades and with clubs and with the particular cruelty of men who have made predation into a trade and are good at it.

I went under the table. That was my first thought — the table, the dark, the hiding, the same instinct that had put me in the ditch and in the grove and in the shadow of the tree, the instinct the Nullwood had taught me, the instinct that said *make yourself small and the danger will pass.* I went under the table and I pulled my knees to my chest and I pressed my back to the wall and I held my breath and I waited for the danger to pass.

It did not pass. The common room was full of noise and movement and the sound of things breaking, and a man was shouting — the reavers' leader, a big man with a voice that carried over the noise the way a bell carries over a field — and the travelers were being pushed to the center of the room, the family, the teamsters, the dwarves, and the reavers were moving through them with the patient efficiency of men sorting livestock, and I was under the table with my knees to my chest and my breath held and my whole body telling me to stay, to hide, to wait, to let the danger pass the way the danger had passed in the ditch and in the grove.

And then I saw Corwin.

He had not gone under the table. He had not gone anywhere. He was standing in the center of the common room with his back to the wall and his hands empty and his face doing the thing I had seen it do in the yard — the closing, the shutting, the door pulled shut behind the eyes — except that now the door was not shutting out a girl. It was shutting out a room full of men with blades, and the shutting was not a closing but an opening, an opening into something I had not seen in him before and would see many times after, something that was not courage and not recklessness but something older and colder and more practiced than either — the particular calm of a man who has done this before, and done it again, and done it again, and is tired of it, and will do it again, and knows he will do it again, and has made his peace with the doing.

The first reaver came at him. A big man, a club, the kind of swing that would have broken me in half. Corwin moved. Not away. *In.* He stepped into the swing the way you step into a door you have opened a thousand times, and his hand came up and caught the man's wrist and turned it, and the club went down, and the man went down with it, and Corwin's other hand came up and the man was still, and the whole of it took the space of one breath, and I watched it from under the table and I understood, in that one breath, that the man I had followed across the frontier was not what I had thought he was, and that what he was was something I did not have a word for.

The second came. A blade. Corwin took it — not the blade, the *man*, the man with the blade — and the man went down the way the first man had gone down, quickly, efficiently, without heat, and Corwin moved to the next, and the next, and the room was noise and blood and the sound of things breaking, and he moved through it the way a man moves through a thing he has done ten thousand times, economical, ruthless, and *tired*, so tired, the tiredness of a man who has fought this fight in this room in this way more times than he can count and is doing it again because the doing is the only thing he knows how to do and the only thing that keeps the people behind him alive.

I came out from under the table. I do not remember deciding to. I remember the table and then the room, the noise and then the moving, and I was on my feet and I was moving, not toward the fight — I was not foolish enough to move toward the fight — but toward the sleepers, the family, the teamsters, the people who were being pushed to the center of the room and did not know what was happening, and I was shouting, the loud kind, the kind the Nullwood had unlearned, and I was pulling, and I was dragging, and I was doing the small thing I could do, which was not fighting but moving, moving the people who could not move themselves toward the door and the dark and the yard and the road, and the reavers were busy with Corwin and the dwarves and the teamsters who had found their nerve, and I was moving, and the moving was the thing, and the thing was enough.

A reaver saw me. A small one, young, the kind of reaver who has not yet learned that the small things are the things that matter, and he came at me with a blade and I did the thing the Nullwood had taught me — I went still, I went small, I made myself part of the wall — and he passed me, his eyes on the fight, his blade on the fight, and I was behind him and I was alive and I was moving and the door was open and the family was through and the teamsters were through and the dwarves were holding the center and Corwin was in the middle of it all, moving through the reavers the way a man moves through a thing he has done ten thousand times, and I was at the door and I was pulling the last of the sleepers through and the night was cold and the yard was dark and the road was south and I was alive and the fight was behind me and Corwin was in it and I could not see him and I could hear him and the hearing was enough.

---

It ended the way these things end — not with a last stand, not with a hero's blow, but with the reavers deciding, one by one, that the waystation was not worth the cost, and the cost was Corwin, and Corwin was not a cost they were willing to pay, and they went out into the dark the way they had come in, quickly, efficiently, with the particular efficiency of men who have made predation into a trade and know when the trade is not worth the doing.

The common room was quiet after. The kind of quiet that comes after noise, the ringing quiet, the quiet that is not silence but the absence of sound, and I stood in the doorway with my hands shaking and my breath coming and my whole body telling me that I had survived something I had not expected to survive, and I looked into the room and I saw the dead and the wounded and the overturned tables and the blood on the floor, and I saw Corwin.

He was sitting on a bench against the wall. His back was to the room. His head was down. And his left hand was pressed to his side, and the hand was red, and the bench was red, and the floor under the bench was red, and he was sitting the way a man sits when he has been hurt badly and is waiting for the hurt to stop, and the hurt was not stopping, and he was waiting anyway, because waiting was the only thing left to do.

I went to him. I knelt beside the bench and I put my hand on his arm and I said, "Let me see."

He looked at me. The door behind his eyes was open now, not the crack but the whole door, and behind it I saw the thing I had not seen before — the exhaustion, the enormous, ancient, bottomless exhaustion of a man who has been hurt too many times and has learned to sit with the hurt the way you sit with a thing you cannot change, and he said, "It is nothing."

"It's not nothing."

"It is nothing. I have had worse."

"Let me see."

He let me. He moved his hand, and I saw the wound — a blade, deep, in the side, the kind of wound that should have dropped him, the kind of wound that should have had him on the floor and not on the bench, and I looked at it and I looked at him and I said, "You should be dead."

"I know," he said. And then, quieter, the dry warm voice, the voice that held the warmth at arm's length: "I have been told."

---

I tended it in the grey of the morning, when the dead had been counted and the wounded had been seen to and the waystation had been made as whole as a waystation can be made after a night like that. I had found cloth. I had found water. I had found the particular calm that comes when the worst thing has happened and you are still here and the doing is the only thing left, and I knelt beside him and I cleaned the wound and I bound it and I watched it do the thing that I would watch it do many times in the years that followed and would never stop finding strange.

It was closing. Already. In the grey morning, six hours after the blade had gone in, the wound was closing — not healing, not the slow patient healing of a body doing what bodies do, but *closing*, the edges drawing together the way a door draws shut, too fast, too clean, as if his body were refusing the injury, as if the flesh itself had decided that this was not a thing it would carry, and I bound it and I sat back and I looked at him and I said, "What."

He looked at me. The door was shut again. The crack was gone. And he said, in the dry warm voice, the voice that was the whole of him and the whole of the distance he kept between himself and the world, "I am a man who got lucky."

"That's not a wound that gets lucky."

"It is when you have had worse."

"You said that last night."

"I have had worse."

I looked at him. He looked at me. And neither of us said what we were both thinking, which was that the wound was not a thing that got lucky, and the closing was not a thing that happened, and the man sitting on the bench with a blade-wound six hours old that was already drawing shut was not a man who got lucky, and the thing he was was something I did not have a word for, and he was not going to give me the word, and I was not going to stop looking for it, and the looking was the thing that would walk between us for the rest of my life, and the looking was the bond, and the bond was the thing that would hold us together across the years and the losses and the long giving-back of the song, and neither of us knew it yet, and the not-knowing was the mercy.

The morning came. The dead were counted — three travelers, two reavers, a teamster who had stood and fought and had not been fast enough. The wounded were bound. The waystation was made whole, or as whole as a thing can be made after a night like that, and Corwin stood in the yard with his hand on his side and his face doing the thing it did — the closing, the shutting, the door pulled shut behind the eyes — and he looked at me and he said, "You are still here."

"Yes."

"You should go."

"I know."

"You stayed."

"Yes."

He was quiet. The yard was quiet. The horse stood between us with the patient indifference of an animal that has seen this before. And then he said, "We are heading to Karrendeep. The dwarven hold. It is a fortnight and the road is bad."

"I know."

"And after that, north. Back to the settlements. To Thistlebrook."

"I know."

He looked at me for a long time. And then he turned to the horse and he began to tack it, and I stood in the yard and I watched him work, and the silence between us was not the silence of the Nullwood and not the silence of the road and not the silence of the first day, but a fourth kind of silence, the silence of two people who have seen each other in the dark and cannot unsee what they have seen, and who will walk on together because the walking is the only thing left to do, and the doing is enough, and the enough is the beginning.
