# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Interlude — The Road Between

---

I have tried, many times, to remember the road between Greymarch and Karrendeep, and I have never been able to remember it whole. That is the strange thing about the weeks that followed the waystation — they do not sit in my memory the way the Nullwood sits, or the trial, or the hollow. They sit the way a song sits when you have heard it so many times that you can no longer tell the notes from the spaces between them. I know we walked. I know the country changed around us, the scrubland giving way to low hills, the low hills giving way to the first hard shoulders of the dwarven country, the sky opening wider and wider until I stopped flinching at the weight of it. But the walking itself has gone soft in my keeping, the way a stone goes soft in a river, and what I have left is not the road but the *shape* of the road — the shape of a girl becoming something she did not yet have a name for.

I know this much: the world taught me what the Nullwood could not. Not Corwin — he was not a teacher, not then, not yet, and he would have laughed at the word if you had offered it to him, the dry unwilling laugh of a man who has been called many things and never the right one. The world taught me. The loud world, the open world, the world that did not fold and did not hush and did not care what you said or how loudly you said it. I had spent my whole life inside a people who had made silence into a virtue, and now I was walking through a country where silence was not a virtue but a vulnerability, where the people who survived were the people who spoke — who asked for directions, who traded stories for bread, who called out to strangers across a field and were answered, and the answering was not a crime but a courtesy.

It undid me. Not all at once. Not in any way I could have named at the time. But the road undid me the way water undoes a knot — patiently, persistently, a little at a time, until the thing that had been held tight is held no longer and you cannot remember what shape it was before the loosening.

---

I remember the waystations. Not all of them — there were many, and they blur together the way waystations do, each one a stone building with a fire and a woman or a man behind a counter and the smell of stew and the sound of strangers talking — but I remember the *fact* of them. That they existed. That the world had built, along its roads, small places where a traveler could stop and be warm and be fed and be spoken to, and that this was not a kindness but an economy, a thing as ordinary as a well or a bridge, and that I had grown up in a world that had no equivalent because the Nullwood had no strangers and no roads and no need of places where strangers could meet and be warm.

I remember the tale-debt. I remember the first time I paid for a meal with a story, and the shock of it — that a thing I had been exiled for doing in secret was, out here, a *currency*, a thing people traded as casually as coin, and that the trading was not a crime but the oldest economy in the world, older than the Nullwood, older than the Quiet, older than the forgetting, and that I had been starving in the midst of a feast I had been taught not to see.

I remember Corwin watching me tell my first tale-debt, and the look on his face — not surprise, not approval, but something smaller and stranger, the look of a man who has been looking for something a very long time and has just seen it, briefly, in a place he did not expect to find it, and is not yet ready to believe what he has seen.

---

I remember the silence between us softening. Not into speech — Corwin was not a man who softened into speech, and I was not yet a woman who knew how to be silent without the silence being a kind of folding — but into something else, something I have only ever been able to call *company*. The particular quiet of two people who are walking the same road and have agreed, without saying so, that the walking is better together than alone. It is not friendship. It is not love. It is the thing that comes before both of those, the thing that makes both of those possible, the small unspoken agreement that you will not leave each other at the next crossroads, and the next, and the next, until the not-leaving becomes a habit, and the habit becomes a bond, and the bond becomes the thing you carry when the road runs out and the leaving comes anyway.

I have carried that bond across ages. I carry it still. It is the oldest thing in my keeping, older than Fenn, older than the hollow, older than the Quiet — because it is the first thing I ever kept that was not a dead thing. It is the first living thing I held, and held gently, and did not fold away, and the holding of it is the thing that taught me, slowly, across the long patience of the road, that keeping is not the same as clinging, and that the difference between the two is the difference between a song sung aloud and a song locked in a box, and that the singing is the thing, and the singing is the gift, and the gift is the only thing that survives the singer.

But I am ahead of myself. I am always ahead of myself, in the telling. That is the curse of the Memory-Keeper — that you know the end of the song before you have finished singing the beginning, and that the knowing bleeds into the telling the way dawn bleeds into the dark, and you can never quite separate the two, and you learn, in time, that the separation was never the point.

The point was the road. The point was the walking. The point was the weeks that have gone soft in my keeping, the weeks between the waystation and the daughter-hold, when the world was large and the road was long and the girl I had been was walking toward the woman I would become, and the walking was the becoming, and the becoming was the thing, and the thing was enough.

It was enough. I want you to know that. Before the road turned, before the found family formed, before the woman arrived who would break the song open and let the light in and make the light worth the breaking — before all of that, there were weeks, and the weeks were good, and the goodness of them is the thing I have kept longest, because it is the thing I did not know I was keeping until it was already gone.

The road went on. We went on with it. And somewhere ahead of us, in a dwarven hold we had not yet reached, a vein of ore was singing wrong, and a beast was stirring in the dark, and a prospector with a grumbling mouth and a kind heart was waiting to fall in with us and make us, without knowing it, a family.

But that is the rest of the song. This was still the between. This was still the road, and the walking, and the soft slow undoing of a girl who had been taught that silence was mercy and was learning, one waystation at a time, that the mercy was in the speaking, and that the speaking was the thing the world had been doing all along, loudly, openly, without shame, and that the world was not wrong, and that the Nullwood was.

We walked on.
