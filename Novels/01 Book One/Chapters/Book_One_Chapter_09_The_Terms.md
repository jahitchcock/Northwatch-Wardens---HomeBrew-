# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Chapter Nine — The Terms

---

He gave me the terms on the second day south of Greymarch, when the road had settled into the particular rhythm of a road that goes nowhere interesting and knows it, and the country had opened into the long scrubland that the frontier called the Flats, and the horse had found its pace and I had found mine beside it, and the silence between us had softened enough that he could speak without the speaking costing more than he was willing to pay.

"I have rules," he said. He did not look at me. He was watching the horizon, the way he always watched the horizon, with the particular attention of a man who has learned that the horizon is where trouble comes from and has made a habit of watching it the way other men make a habit of prayer. "If you are coming with me, you follow them."

I said I would listen.

"First. I do not explain myself. Where I am going, why I am going, what I am looking for — that is mine. You do not ask, and if you ask I will not answer, and if I do not answer you do not ask again." He paused. "Second. I do not talk about where I have been. You will see things. You will notice things. You will want to know. You do not ask." Another pause, longer this time, the pause of a man reaching for the third rule and finding it harder than the others, because the third rule was the one that cost. "Third. If I tell you to run, you run. Not after. Not when you are sure. *When I say.* You run, and you do not look back, and you do not come back for me."

He looked at me then. The door behind his eyes was open — not the crack, not the whole door, but a space, a narrow space, the width of a man who is offering a thing he does not expect you to take and is bracing for the taking.

"That is it," he said. "Three rules. You can break any of them and I will leave you at the next settlement and you will never see me again. Those are the terms."

I looked at him. I looked at the horizon he had been watching, the long empty line where the scrubland met the sky and the sky met nothing, and I thought about the rules, and I thought about the man who had made them, and I thought about the girl I had been when I walked out of the Nullwood, the girl who had followed a stranger across the frontier because she could not bear to walk toward nothing, and I thought: *I will break every one of them.*

Not today. Not tomorrow. Not for a long time, if I was careful, and if I was lucky, and if the world was kind. But I would break them. I knew it the way you know a thing in the body before the mind has named it — that the need to know is the thing I was made of, the thing the Nullwood had tried to fold and had failed to fold, the thing that had got me exiled and would get me into worse before the song was done, and that the man beside me on the horse had made his rules out of the same need I had made my archive out of — the need to hold a thing so tightly that no one else can reach it, and the believing, against all evidence, that the holding is enough.

"I agree," I said.

He looked at me. He knew. I am certain now that he knew — that he saw in my face the same thing Ilvath had seen in the ring, the thing that was not a lie but was not the truth either, the thing that was a girl agreeing to a thing she intended to break and believing, in the agreeing, that the intention was enough to make the breaking forgivable. He knew, and he let it pass, the way Ilvath had let it pass, the way the gentle always let it pass, because the letting-pass is the only kindness they have left to give when the kindness is the thing that will undo them.

"Then come," he said. And we walked on.

---

The road to Karrendeep was a fortnight, and in those two weeks I learned the shape of the man I had followed. Not the whole of him — the whole of him would take years, and the years would take the whole of me, and neither of us would come out of the learning unchanged — but the shape, the outline, the thing you see when you look at a person from the side and catch the profile before the face turns away.

He was kind. That was the thing I had not expected, and the thing I have spent the longest time learning to hold. I had expected — I do not know what I had expected. Someone harder, someone whose weariness had worn the kindness out of him the way the road wears the tread off a boot. He was not hard. He was tired, and the tiredness was old, and the oldness was the kind that comes from carrying a thing too long, and the carrying had not made him cruel but had made him careful, careful in the way that a man is careful when he has broken too many things and has learned to move through the world as though everything in it might break if he touches it.

He showed me the kindness on the second day, in a small way, the way the kind always show it — not in the grand gesture but in the unguarded one, the thing done without thought and noticed only after. We had stopped at a stream to water the horse, and I was sitting on the bank with my boots off and my feet in the cold water, and he was tending the wound — his wound, the one that had closed too fast, the one he would not let me look at again — and when he was done he came to the bank and he sat beside me, not close, not far, the distance he always kept, and he took from his pack a small thing wrapped in cloth and he unwrapped it and it was bread, the hard kind, the kind the frontier bakes for the road, and he broke it in half and he gave me the larger piece and he ate the smaller one and he did not say anything about it and I did not say anything about it and we sat on the bank and ate our bread in the quiet that was not the Quiet and the quiet was good and the bread was good and the stream ran over the stones and the horse drank and the world was the world and the world was kind, in its way, in the small way that the world is kind when it offers you a man who breaks his bread in half and gives you the larger piece and does not say anything about it.

That was Corwin. That was the whole of him, in the small things, in the unguarded moments, in the kindnesses he did not know he was doing and would have denied if you had named them, because the denying was the thing he did with the kindnesses the way the Nullwood did with the names — folded them away, tucked them into a place he did not have to open, and went on, and let the folding stand for the feeling, and believed, against all evidence, that the standing-for was enough.

---

It was near the end of the second week, in the late afternoon, with Karrendeep a day ahead and the scrubland giving way to the first low hills of the dwarven country, that I heard it.

He was riding ahead of me, the way he always rode, a little ahead, the distance he kept, and I was walking beside the horse's flank with my eyes on the road and my mind on the road and the particular nothing that the mind does when the body is walking and the road is long and the road is going nowhere interesting, and under his breath, so faintly that I almost did not catch it, so faintly that it might have been the wind or the creak of the saddle or the sound of my own tired mind making music where there was none — he was humming.

A few notes. Not a melody, not yet, not the kind of thing you could call a song, just a fragment, a scrap, the way you hum when your mind is elsewhere and your mouth is doing a thing your mind is not attending to, and the fragment was — I have tried, many times, to set it down, and I have never been able to set it down the way I heard it, because the hearing was the thing, the hearing was the whole of it, the way a few notes hummed under a man's breath on a road in the late afternoon can land in your ear and stay there, the way a splinter stays, and refuse to be forgotten.

I said, "What is that."

He stopped. Not the horse — *him.* He stopped the way you stop when you have been caught at a thing you did not know you were doing, and he turned in the saddle and looked at me with a face I had not seen before — startled, almost, the startlement of a man who has been alone so long that the sound of his own voice is a surprise to him, and the surprise is not pleasant, and the not-pleasant is the thing that makes him shut the door again, harder, until the next time the door opens without his meaning it to.

"What is what," he said.

"The humming. What were you humming."

He looked at me. The door was open — the narrow space, the width of a man who has been caught and does not know what to do with the catching — and he said, "I was not humming."

"You were."

"I was not." He turned back to the road. "I do not hum."

"You were humming. A few notes. Under your breath. You did not know you were doing it."

He was quiet. The horse walked on. The road went south. And then he said, in the dry warm voice, the voice that was the whole of him and the whole of the distance he kept, "I do not know what it is. I have had it —" he stopped. He started again. "I have had it a long time. I do not know where it comes from."

He did not say anything else. I did not ask anything else. We walked on, and the road went on, and the hills came up, and the afternoon went down, and the fragment stayed in my ear the way a splinter stays, and I filed it, and I did not understand it, and I would not understand it for a long time, and the not-understanding was the mercy, because the understanding, when it came, would come in a room with a dying woman and a song on her lips and a man who would not weep and a girl who would not stop keeping, and the understanding would be the thing that broke the song open and let the light in, and the light would be the thing that made the song worth the breaking.

But that is the rest of the song. This was still the beginning. This was still the road, and the horse, and the man, and the girl, and the fragment of a song that neither of them knew was a song, and the walking on, and the going south, and the not-knowing, and the mercy.

---

We came to the hills that evening, and the hills were the edge of the dwarven country, and beyond the hills was Karrendeep, and beyond Karrendeep was the road north, and beyond the road north was Thistlebrook, and beyond Thistlebrook was the thing Corwin was riding toward and did not know he was riding toward — a woman, though I did not know it yet, who would become the song for both of us, and none of us knew it, and the not-knowing was the mercy, and we walked on.

Corwin named the destination that night, at the fire, in the quiet that was not the Quiet, with the hills behind us and the dwarven country ahead and the stars coming out over a world that was larger and louder and more alive than anything the Nullwood had prepared me for.

"Thistlebrook," he said. "Frontier settlement. North of Karrendeep. I have heard there is someone there who remembers the old songs." He paused. "I have heard that everywhere. Every settlement, every waystation, every boundary I have stopped at. Someone who remembers the old songs. And it is never true, and I go anyway, because the going is the only thing I know how to do, and the only thing that keeps me walking."

He looked at the fire. The fire looked back. And I sat across from him with my hands in my lap and my face open and my silence not folded but held, and I watched him watch the fire, and I thought: *he is looking for the thing I was exiled for. He is looking for the old songs. And he does not know that the old songs are looking for him too.* I did not yet know that the looking would walk between us for the rest of my life — that his story would become my song, and my song would become the thing I spent my whole long life keeping. But I felt it, faintly, the way you feel a word on the tip of the tongue.

I did not say any of that. I said, "I'll come with you to Thistlebrook."

He looked at me. The door was open — the narrow space, the width of a man who has been offered company and has decided, against his better judgment, to accept it. He had been alone so long that the not-being-alone frightened him more than the being-alone ever had, and the frightening was the only thing that still felt like living.

"All right," he said.

And we sat by the fire, and the fire burned, and the stars came out, and the hills held their shape against the dark, and somewhere south of us, though I did not know it yet, in a settlement called Thistlebrook, a woman was humming a fragment of a song she did not know she knew — the same fragment Corwin had hummed on the road. The sameness was the thread. The thread was the song. None of us knew it, and the not-knowing was the mercy. We sat by the fire and we were alive and we were together and we were walking toward something we could not yet name, and the walking was enough.

I had chosen to walk toward the world. That was the whole of it, in the end — a girl, exiled and unmoored, who had chosen to walk toward the loud instead of mourning the quiet, toward the stranger instead of the silence, toward the song instead of the folding. I did not yet know that the choosing would make me what I would become, or that the becoming would be the song I would spend my whole long life keeping. But I knew, in the way you know a thing in the body before the mind has named it, that I would not go back.

But that is the rest of the song. This was still the beginning. This was still the fire, and the stars, and the man, and the girl, and the road south, and the not-knowing, and the mercy, and the enough.

We walked on.
