# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Chapter Twenty-Four — I Have Buried This Song Before

---

We buried her at the edge of the wild miles, where the scrubland gave way to a stand of birch. Maelis chose the spot — a rise of ground with good drainage and a view of the eastern sky. He said the old ones believed the dead should face the dawn.

Brannoc dug. He worked the earth the way dwarves work stone — with patience, with rhythm, with a kind of prayer built into the motion of the blade. He did not speak. He dug until the grave was deep enough and then he dug a little more.

Maelis laid her in. He sang the leaving song — the one he had described to me in the vigil, the one that does not hurry and does not hold. His voice was low and steady. His hands were not.

---

Corwin stood apart.

He had helped with the burial — he had carried stones, had shaped the marker with a precision that spoke of long practice. But when the singing began, he stepped back. Not far. Far enough.

I watched him.

His grief was wrong. I had said that to myself in the vigil and I said it again now, watching him stand at the edge of the grave with his hands at his sides and his face composed. His grief was wrong because it was too large for a love this brief and too familiar for a loss this fresh.

He stood the way a man stands at a graveside he has visited a hundred times. He knew where to put his feet. He knew when to bow his head. He knew the exact moment the singing would end and the silence would begin.

He knew it the way a sailor knows weather.

---

The ceremony ended. Brannoc filled the grave. Maelis placed the marker stone. I spoke Wren's name aloud — the only one of us who would, in the days to come, speak it without flinching.

We stood in the silence that follows a burial. The kind of silence that is not empty but full — full of everything that was said and everything that was not.

Brannoc was the first to move. He wiped his hands on his trousers and looked at the grave and looked away.

"I'll be going, then."

No one answered.

"Back to the hold. Back to stone." He paused. "She'd have wanted me to go home."

"Yes," I said.

He nodded. He did not look at any of us. He walked to the edge of the birch stand and stopped and stood there for a long time, his back to us, his shoulders moving. Then he walked into the trees and was gone.

---

Three of us remained.

Maelis began to break the camp. He moved through the practical tasks with the same quiet efficiency he had brought to the laying-out. He folded the bedrolls. He packed the waterskins. He divided the food.

Corwin sat on the marker stone.

I sat beside him. Not close. Close enough.

We did not speak for a long time. The sun moved across the sky. The shadows lengthened. The birch leaves whispered in a wind I could not feel.

"The Hollow Choir," I said.

He did not look at me.

"It is days from here. You said so yourself. Weeks, perhaps, but not far. We could still go."

He said nothing.

"Corwin."

"I am not going to the Hollow Choir."

I waited.

"I am not going."

---

I did not understand. The Hollow Choir was what he had crossed the world for — through the Thinning, through the wild miles, through reaver country and deep-beast territory. He had fought and bled and kept walking when any mortal man would have stopped. The Hollow Choir was the thing he had chased for — how long? He never said. But the grief in him was older than any grief I had ever witnessed, and the chase had been longer than any chase I could imagine.

And now he was not going.

"Why."

He looked at the grave. His face was still. The composure was not calm — it was the stillness of a man who has made a decision so many times it has become reflex.

"I have buried this song before."

I took it for poetry. A grieving man's metaphor — the song as love, the burial as loss, the before as the long history of loving and losing. I had heard men speak this way at gravesides. I had heard them reach for language that could hold what they felt, and fail, and try again.

I did not press him.

*"I would not understand what he had said for many years. When I did, the floor of the world shifted beneath me, and I had to learn to stand on it again."*

---

Maelis finished the camp. He brought us each a portion of food and a skin of water. He set them down without a word and moved to his own pack.

I watched Corwin eat. He ate the way he did everything — deliberately, without pleasure or complaint, as though eating were a duty and not a comfort. His hands were steady. His face was composed.

The grief was there. I could feel it the way you feel heat from a forge — invisible, immense, radiating from a source you cannot see. But it was a grief he had contained before, a grief he had walked through before, a grief he had learned to carry the way a man learns to carry a wound that will never fully heal.

He had done this before.

I did not know how many times. I did not know how long the road behind him stretched. I only knew that the familiarity frightened me more than the depth.

---

That night, I lay awake and listened to the dark.

Maelis slept the way hunters sleep — lightly, one ear open, ready to wake at the first wrong sound. Corwin did not sleep. I could hear him breathing, slow and even, the breathing of a man who has learned to mimic rest without achieving it.

The Hollow Choir was days away. The oldest songs in the world were waiting in a ruin that Corwin had spent ages seeking. And he had turned his back on it without a word, without a struggle, without even the dignity of a reason.

He no longer wanted the thing he had crossed the world to find.

I did not understand why. I would not understand for a long time. But I felt the shape of it — the shape of a man who has chased something across centuries and finally understood that the chase itself was the trap.
