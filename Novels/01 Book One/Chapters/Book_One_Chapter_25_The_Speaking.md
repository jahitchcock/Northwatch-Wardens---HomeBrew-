# The Old Songs of Aevoria
## Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
### Chapter Twenty-Five — The Speaking

---

The helplessness came back.

I had felt it once before — in the grove, the night Fenn died, when the elders came and took his name from the air and I stood with my mouth full of silence and my hands full of nothing. I had been a child then. I had not understood what was happening. I had only known that something was being taken from me and I could not stop it.

Now I was not a child. Now I understood. And the helplessness was worse.

Wren was gone. The fever had taken her the way the Withering had taken Fenn — not with spectacle, not with war, not with any villain to blame. An ordinary death. The kind the world hands out without ceremony, without meaning, without the decency of a reason.

And the Quiet was waiting for me. I could feel it — the old reflex, the closing of the fist, the instinct to hoard the grief inside where no one could see it and no one could take it away. The Nullwood had taught me well. Keep it in. Keep it safe. Keep it silent.

I refused.

---

I went to the grave.

The marker stone stood in the pale morning light. The birch leaves moved above it. The earth was still fresh, still dark, still turned.

I sat down beside it.

Maelis was packing the last of the camp. Corwin sat on a stone twenty paces away, his back to the grave, his eyes on the eastern horizon. Neither of them watched me.

I opened my mouth.

Nothing came.

The Quiet was strong. Two generations of silence had built it into something more than law — it was architecture, it was the house we lived inside, it was the air we breathed. Speaking a dead name aloud was not just forbidden. It was *unthinkable*. The word for it did not exist because the act did not exist.

I sat there for a long time. The sun moved. The shadows shifted.

Then I thought of Fenn.

I thought of his small hands and his serious face. I thought of the way he had said, *"When the worst thing happens, I will be your knife."* I thought of the grove and the silence and the name I had never been allowed to speak.

And I thought: *No.*

Not this time. Not again. Not her.

---

"Wren."

The name came out of me like water from a spring — not forced, not wrenched, but released. The first drop of something that had been held too long.

"Wren of Thistlebrook."

I said it again. Louder. Letting the birch trees hear it. Letting the earth hear it. Letting the sky hear it.

"Wren, who could read the land the way I read memory. Who saw the shape of a thing before the thing showed itself. Who was never afraid — not of the road, not of the dark, not of the fever that took her."

The words came faster now. Not the hoarded whisper of the name-slip, not the secret keeping of the hollow in the tree. This was different. This was open. This was a giving-away.

"She laughed at Brannoc's jokes even when they were not funny. She sang in the mornings. She named people directly — *Elowen, Maelis, Corwin* — the way you name someone when you want them to know they are seen."

I was crying. I did not stop.

"Warm. Quick. Fearless. She loved the world the way the world deserves to be loved — completely, without reservation, without the caution that the rest of us hide behind."

The tears ran down my face and fell on the fresh earth and the earth took them the way it takes rain.

"She hummed a song she did not know she knew. The oldest song. The one from before. And when she died, the song did not die with her. It is still here. I can still hear it."

I stopped.

The silence that followed was not the Quiet. The Quiet was an absence — a silence built by fear, maintained by law, enforced by grief that had nowhere to go. This silence was something else. This silence was full. This silence was the space a voice leaves behind when it has said what it needed to say.

---

Something eased.

Not in the world — the world did not change. The grave was still a grave. Wren was still dead. The fever had still done its work and the earth had still received her and nothing could undo what had been done.

But something eased in me. The grief was still there — vast, heavy, the kind of grief that sits in the chest like a stone and does not move. But it was lighter. Not because it was smaller, but because it was no longer mine alone. I had spoken it into the air and the air had carried it and the carrying had made it survivable.

Grief spoken becomes grief shared.

I had not known this. I had spent my whole life believing the opposite — that keeping was safety, that silence was strength, that a thing held close was a thing preserved. The Nullwood had taught me this. The Quiet had enforced it. The Withering had made it law.

But the Quiet was wrong.

Some songs must be given away to live.

---

Corwin had turned.

I did not know when. I did not know how long he had been watching. But when I looked up from the grave, he was facing me. His expression was one I had never seen on his face before — not grief, not composure, not the ancient weariness I had come to know.

Wonder.

He was watching a mortal woman do, in a single morning, the one thing he had never managed in all his ages. He was watching me keep a thing by giving it away.

He did not speak. He did not move. He simply watched, and the watching was its own kind of recognition.

---

Maelis came to the edge of the grave.

He stood there for a moment, looking at me. His face was unreadable — the face of a man who has spent his life in the quiet spaces between words and is now hearing words fill those spaces for the first time.

"Say it again," he said.

"Wren."

"Again."

"Wren of Thistlebrook."

He nodded. Slowly. As though the name were a physical thing and he was learning its weight.

"I could not say it," he said. "I tried. In the night. I tried and the words would not come."

"I know."

"Say it for me."

I looked at him. The hunter. The man who had loved Wren and never said so. The second man who had lost her.

"Wren," I said. "Maelis loved you. He loved you the way the old ones love — quietly, without expectation, without the need for return. He would have followed you to the edge of the world and beyond it, and you knew that, and you loved him for it, even though you could not love him back the way he deserved."

Maelis closed his eyes.

"She is kept," I said. "She is spoken. She will not be lost."

He opened his eyes. They were bright.

"That is enough," he said.

*"I did not know it then, but I had just founded something that would outlast me — a practice, a discipline, a way of keeping that would spread across the Nullwood and beyond it, carried by voices I would never hear."*
