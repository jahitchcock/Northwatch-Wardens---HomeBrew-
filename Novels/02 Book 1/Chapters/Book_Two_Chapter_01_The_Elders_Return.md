# The Elders Return

I sat in the Memory Grove, the old trees whispering their names as I spoke them aloud. The wind was quiet, and the sky had not yet darkened. I had been waiting for something I could not name, though I did not know it then. The grove had always been my place — where I listened to the echoes of those who came before, where I kept their songs in my bones. But tonight, there was a weight in the air that made me stop, as if the world itself had paused.

He arrived at dusk, as he always did. Corwin walked through the trees like a shadow cast by something unseen, his steps slow and deliberate. I did not look up immediately — I knew him well enough to feel when he was near. The grove had grown quiet in his presence, as if it remembered him.

He sat beside me without speaking, his back straight but his hands folded loosely in his lap. He looked older than the last time I saw him, though I could not say how much time had passed since we last met. There was a weight on him now — not of age, but of something deeper, something that had been carried for years and never released.

I reached for the kettle, my fingers moving as if they had always known what to do. The fire caught with a quiet crackle, and I poured tea into two cups. We did not speak at first. There was no need. We both knew why we were here.

Finally, he looked up. His eyes met mine, and for the first time in many years, I saw something in them that had not been there before — a grief older than memory, heavier than any loss I had known.

“I have never told anyone this story,” he said, his voice low. “It is the oldest thing I carry. The one that shaped everything after.”

I nodded slowly. “Then tell me.” 

He exhaled, as if releasing something long held in. His fingers brushed the rim of his cup, and for a moment, I saw him not just as Corwin, but as the man who had walked east toward the road with no end. The one who had returned only when the world turned.

The fire caught again, brighter now, casting shadows across the grove. And then he began to speak — his voice trembling at first, like a song that had not been sung in many years. I listened as the Age of Dragons rose around us, and for a moment, I was no longer in the Memory Grove.

He told me how it all began, how the sky had once been filled with dragons whose wings carved the clouds into shapes that could never be named. How mortals had looked up at them and wondered if they were gods — or if something far older still watched from above.

I listened as he spoke of the council at Skyreach, where elves, dwarves, and humans gathered to decide what to do with the dragons. I heard the voices of Valdris, Sereveth, Kazir, and Aerim, each one speaking for their people and their beliefs. I saw Corwin standing apart from them all, watching as they debated whether to bind the dragons or leave them free.

And I felt the weight of what he was about to tell me — how the first bonds were made, how the sky changed when mortals took it upon themselves to shape the world in their own image. How the dragons no longer flew alone, but shared their breath with those who had once only looked up at them.

I did not interrupt him. I did not need to. He was speaking now, and I was listening — not as a Memory-Keeper, but as someone who had waited for this moment all her life.

The grove fell away as the Age of Dragons rose around us. And in that moment, I knew: this story would be the one I carried forever.

I sat beneath the old trees, my fingers tracing the rim of my cup as Corwin spoke. The fire crackled softly between us, casting long shadows across the grove, and for a moment, it seemed as if the world had turned back to something older than time itself. 

He told me how he had walked east, drawn by an unseen force that pulled him toward the sky where dragons once flew without care. He spoke of the journey through mist-laden forests, where echoes of ancient songs lingered in the wind like ghosts of a forgotten age. I listened as his voice grew steadier, and with it, the story began to take shape — not just as words on my tongue, but as something that had been waiting for me all along.

He described the fortress of Skyreach, its towers reaching toward the heavens like fingers grasping at the stars. There, among elves, dwarves, and humans, he had seen the first stirrings of what would become the Dragon Pact — a fragile promise between mortals and dragons that neither side fully understood. He spoke of Valdris, the scholar-warrior who saw in the pact not just a bond, but an opportunity to shape the world as he saw fit. And I felt the weight of his words settle over me like dust on old parchment.

He told me how Sereveth had stood at the edge of the council hall, her icy gaze sharp with caution, and how Kazir had spoken of the forge’s fire, of the strength that came from forging something new from what was broken. I could see them in my mind — not as they were now, but as they had been then, before the pact had begun to unravel.

And then there was Aerim, the High Priest who carried the voice of the gods and the weight of their will. He had spoken of divine blessing, of a covenant that would bring peace to both mortals and dragons alike. But I knew, even then, that such things were never so simple as they seemed.

Corwin’s voice wavered as he spoke of his own role in it all — how he had stood apart from the others, watching as they debated what could not be undone. He told me of the question he had asked, the one that had cut through the ceremony like a blade: *“What binds a dragon to a mortal, and what does a broken binding leave behind?”* I could almost hear the silence that followed, how it had stretched between them all like an unspoken truth.

He did not say much about himself beyond that. He spoke of the choice he had made — to walk away from the pact, to refuse the power that others so eagerly sought. And though I could not see his face, I felt the weight of what he was carrying in his silence.

I watched the fire as it flickered between us, and for a moment, I thought of Wren. Of how she had once carried a melody that had never been meant for her to know. How the song had followed her through every step she took, until at last, it had found its way back to me.

I did not speak as he finished his story. There was no need. The grove had grown quiet around us, and I could feel the weight of what he had told me settle over me like a shadow stretching across the sky.

He looked up then, his eyes dark with something I could not name. “It is the oldest thing I carry,” he said again, almost to himself. “The one that shaped everything after.”

I nodded slowly. “Then tell me.” 

And so, as the fire burned low and the stars began to rise above the trees, I listened — not just as a Memory-Keeper, but as someone who had waited for this moment all her life.

He told me of the first bonds, how Valdris had chosen Vorath, the bronze dragon whose scales shimmered like molten gold beneath the sun. He spoke of Sereveth and Nyraeth, the ice-blue dragon who moved with the grace of wind through the trees, and Kazir and Thurum, the stone-dragon whose presence was as heavy as the mountains themselves.

I could see them in my mind — not just as they were now, but as they had been then. I saw Valdris standing before the council, his hands outstretched as he reached for power that had never belonged to him. I heard Sereveth’s voice, sharp and clear, warning of what was to come. And I felt Kazir’s fire in every word he spoke, a warmth that could not be denied.

But it was Corwin who carried the weight of it all — not just the story, but the truth behind it. He had watched as others bound themselves to dragons, their hands trembling with fear and hope. He had seen the moment when the first bond was made, how the dragon’s eyes had darkened with something that could not be named.

And I felt it too — the shift in the air, the way the sky had changed when mortals had taken it upon themselves to shape the world in their own image. The dragons no longer flew alone, but shared their breath with those who had once only looked up at them.

He spoke of how Valdris had pushed beyond what was meant to be done, how he had experimented with deeper bonds and found something that should never have been discovered. I could feel the tension in his voice as he described Vorath’s madness, how the dragon had gone mad with pain and crashed into the valley below.

I saw it then — not just in words, but in the way the grove seemed to shift around us, as if the very air was remembering what had been lost. I could almost hear the echoes of the past, the way they lingered like dust on old parchment, waiting for someone to speak them aloud.

And still, Corwin did not stop. He told me of the council’s fracture, how Kazir had confronted him with the question he had always known — *“You knew.”* I could feel the weight of that moment in the silence between us, as if the fire itself was holding its breath.

He spoke of his refusal to bind a dragon, of how he had stood apart from the others and watched as they made their choices. He told me of Rynn, the young wayfinder who had arrived at Skyreach with a melody she could not explain, and how she had drawn him in like a song that had been waiting for her all along.

I listened as his voice grew steady once more, as if he were no longer speaking to me, but to something far older than either of us. I felt the weight of what he was carrying settle over me like dust on old parchment, and I knew that this story would be the one I carried forever.
