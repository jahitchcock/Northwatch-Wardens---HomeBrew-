# The Old Songs of Aevoria
### Series Outline & Bible

*A five-book narrative fiction series set in the deep history of Aevoria, designed to stand alone as fiction while giving players and DMs a felt sense of the world's myth, gods, and ages — entirely separate from current campaign events.*

---

## 1. Series Concept

**Genre & Comps:** Epic fantasy / mythic fiction. Influences: *Dragonlance* (Weis & Hickman), *Legend of Drizzt* (R.A. Salvatore), *The Death Gate Cycle* (Weis & Hickman), *The Dark Tower* (Stephen King) — specifically Roland's deathless, singular quest across ages.

**Premise:** Long before the world of the current campaign, Aevoria passed through a series of mythic ages — the breaking of the world, the age of dragons, the fall of a great dwarven hold, the drowning of an elder kingdom. These are not told as a textbook. They are told as **legend**, refracted through the native magic and memory-traditions of each culture that lived them.

**The Anchor:** A single deathless man, **Corwin**, walks through every age. He cannot die — he was denied his ending by the **Ash-Father**, god of endings and rest, as punishment for refusing to let go of the woman he loved at the moment the world broke. He has spent every age since searching for her, because she keeps returning — never as the same face, always taken too soon. No culture remembers him as one man; each names him something different out of their own legend (the Unending, Ashforgotten, the Long Walker). Only the reader, and one other constant — **Elowen**, the first Memory-Keeper — know it is always the same man.

**The Throughline:** Corwin's lost love, **Wren**, is reincarnated across every age and lost again in every book, carried forward by a fragment of song she half-remembers but cannot place. The full depth of this — that it is one soul, one curse, and that Corwin caused it by refusing the Ash-Father — is not revealed until Book Four. Book Five (the capstone) shows the origin: the Sundering itself, the love before the break, and the refusal that started it all.

**Series Title Meaning:** *The Old Songs of Aevoria* operates on two levels — the myths and legends of the world, **and** literally Wren's song, the oldest one, predating the broken world.

**Firewall Rule:** Nothing in this series references Aeor, the dimensional rift, the Aeorian Echo, Eclipse Day, or any current campaign plot device. These are native Aevoria gods, native magic traditions, and deep-time legend only. The goal is texture and history a player or DM can read or listen to independently of the campaign.

## 2. The Anchor Cast

### Corwin — *the Unending, Ashforgotten, the Long Walker*
A deathless wanderer cursed at the Sundering when he refused the Ash-Father's mercy and would not let his love be taken into rest. Warm, not brooding — his tragedy is that he keeps loving people across the ages knowing he will bury every one of them. Plainly human in name; mythic only in what cultures call him after he's gone. His arc across the series bends from **hope** (I can save her, keep her, break this curse) through **grinding grief** to, by Book Four, the unbearable understanding that the chase itself is the trap.

### Wren — the lost love
Reincarnated in every age, taken too soon every time, carried by a song she half-knows but cannot place. Read as a *separate* tragic woman in Books 1–3. The truth — one soul, one curse — breaks open in Book Four. Her true name and origin are revealed in Book Five.

### Elowen — the Memory-Keeper, our narrator
Born as a character in Book One; becomes the first of a line of Memory-Keepers who narrate every subsequent age. Elven memory-magic made literal: to keep a memory is to speak it aloud so it is never lost. She is the only one who knows Corwin is the same man across every age — but for most of the series she does **not** realize the women are the same soul; memory cannot catch what only love can hold. She loves Corwin slowly, quietly, across the ages, in the shadow of a ghost she can't compete with — a love he is structurally unable to return while he is still chasing Wren.

### Thematic Spine
**Elowen holds. Corwin releases — or refuses to.** Her gift is keeping; his curse is being kept. Their disagreement — must the past be held, or let go? — is the philosophical heartbeat of the cycle, and it resolves only at the capstone.

## 3. The Mechanism: How the Reveal Builds

| Book | What the reader believes | What's actually happening |
|---|---|---|
| **One** | An immortal loves and loses a mortal woman — the price of deathlessness. Standalone tragedy. | First instance the reader witnesses. Corwin: *"I have buried this song before."* Filed as poetry. |
| **Two** | A second, unrelated tragic love, lost to the war. | The song recurs. An attentive reader feels resonance. |
| **Three** | A third loss — this time a near-miss, barely glimpsed. | Elowen confesses her gift has a blind spot around these women. Suspicion peaks. |
| **Four** | — | **Full reveal.** One soul. One curse. Corwin's refusal at the root of it. The song is proof. Every prior loss recontextualizes at once. Emotional climax of the series. |
| **Five** | — | **Origin.** The Sundering. The love before the break. The refusal that started it all. Wren's true name. The song's first singing. Resolution. |

**The Token:** A melody, not a face or mark — sung or half-known by Wren in every life, taught to her by Corwin before the world broke. Doubles as native song-magic (canon), and as an audiobook device: a leitmotif a narrator can let the listener recognize before the characters do.

## 4. Book-by-Book Outline

### Book One — *She Who Would Not Be Silent*
**Region/Culture:** The Nullwood — origin of the elves' Quiet Age and spoken-memory magic.
**Comp:** Legend of Drizzt (wanderer's heart, found family, frontier of a new world).

After a great grief, the elves of the Nullwood turn inward, choosing silence over the pain of speaking loss aloud. A young apprentice — **Elowen** — refuses to let memory die with silence. Cast out, she takes to the road and meets **Corwin**, a stranger who has clearly walked farther and lost more than anyone she's known. He becomes her teacher; she becomes the reason he remembers what memory is *for*. Along the way the reader tours the elder world through her eyes — dwarves who sing to stone, the first whispers of dragon-riders on the horizon, gods who still walk close to mortal lands.

Mid-book, Corwin loves and loses a woman — **the reader's first sight of Wren**, though no one names the pattern yet. Elowen witnesses his grief and reads it as simple loneliness.

**Ending:** Elowen becomes the first Memory-Keeper, carrying forward the discipline that narrates every subsequent book. Both anchors, and the throughline, are born in this volume.

**Tone:** Lyrical, melancholy, intimate, propulsive.

---

### Book Two — *The Dragon-Sworn*
**Region/Culture:** The Age of Dragons — origin of the first mortal-dragon pact, and why dragons became myth.
**Comp:** Dragonlance (ensemble tragedy, heroic war, hubris and its cost).

An ensemble of mismatched companions — among them a brilliant, bitter figure tempted to turn sacred pact into weapon — bind themselves to dragons for the first time, blessed by the gods. Corwin walks among them as the one man who cannot bind a dragon, an outsider who sees the hubris forming before anyone else will admit it. The war is glorious, then it is grief: the first pact is broken, and that breaking is why dragons recede into legend and rarity for every age that follows.

**Wren returns**, new face, tangled in the war — and is taken by the very pact Corwin warned against. The first deliberate resonance: the song recurs.

**Ending:** Dragons pass into myth. Corwin walks away from the war's wreckage having lost her again — and the reader starts to notice.

**Tone:** Heroic, tragic, grand.

---

### Book Three — *The Stone That Remembers*
**Region/Culture:** Tharundor, the great dwarven hold — origin of resonance-magic and geomancy.
**Comp:** A dwarven *Pompeii* — Moria-as-a-full-novel, awe and loss, dual-timeline structure.

The rise and fall of a glorious deep hold under the Stonefather, told in two braided timelines — the hold's golden age, and its last day. A stonesinger hears a warning in the rock that the hold's pride won't let anyone heed: they have delved into something that should have stayed silent. Corwin arrives as the one stranger the dwarves distrust, and becomes the only soul who walks *out* of the collapse, carrying its grief because he's the only one left to carry it.

**Wren is here too** — but this time it is a near-miss: they barely find each other before the hold falls, or she dies in the collapse moments after they meet. The cruelest variation yet.

**Elowen's confession:** narrating this age, she admits her gift has a blind spot — she cannot hold these particular women in memory; they slip from even her keeping. Suspicion peaks for the reader.

**Ending:** The hold falls. Dwarven grief over the disaster becomes the seed of their stubborn, tradition-bound culture in every age after.

**Tone:** Claustrophobic, elegiac, grand-then-buried.

---

### Book Four — *The Drowned Kingdom*
**Region/Culture:** An elder, native, sunken civilization — origin of tide- and storm-magic.
**Comp:** Death Gate Cycle (a whole realm descended through, a wrongness beneath everything) with Lovecraftian undertow.

An ancient native kingdom by the sea worships a drowned or oath-bound god. Corwin walks its streets after the sea finally takes everything — the central image of the book. Here, the full architecture breaks open: across one final loss, or one impossible moment of memory before an ending, the truth assembles — **one soul, one curse, and Corwin's own refusal at the Sundering as its root.** Elowen, narrating, finally connects the pattern she's carried without seeing across three prior ages. The song is named for what it always was.

**This is the emotional climax of the series**, though not its chronological one. Every loss in Books 1–3 recontextualizes in the reader's mind simultaneously.

**Ending:** Corwin and Elowen both know, fully, for the first time, what he has been doing to himself — and to her — for every age since the Sundering.

**Tone:** Eerie, mythic, devastating; water's long patience as horror and grief at once.

---

### Capstone — Book Five — *The Sundering of the Whole*
**Region/Culture:** The world's origin myth — the gods, the breaking, the source of every native magic tradition.
**Comp:** A short, lyrical cosmology-as-revelation — the Silmarillion register, but earned rather than front-loaded.

The oldest song the Memory-Keepers carry: how the world was once whole, how the gods — the Radiant Queen, the Stonefather, the Verdant Mother, the Flame-Mother and Ash-Father — shaped and then broke it, and how every magic the reader has now watched across four books (song, stone, dragon-bond, tide) is a shard of that first wholeness.

At its center: **Wren's true name. Corwin's defiance of the Ash-Father. The love before the break.** The first singing of the song. Read last, after the reader has felt the entire long consequence, the first time we see Wren alive and whole is unbearable — because we already know everything that follows.

**Resolution (the fork — decide at drafting):**
- **Option A — Release:** Corwin finally does the one thing he's never done — lets her go into the Ash-Father's rest, not by clinging but by releasing. This may open the door to Elowen.
- **Option B — Cost:** Releasing Wren means Corwin can finally end too — so choosing to let go of one love means surrendering deathlessness, and possibly Elowen, entirely.
- **Option C — Ambiguous:** Leave the resolution a half-step from certain.

**Tone:** Mythic, intimate, quietly devastating; the only place pure cosmology belongs, because it now lands as revelation, not exposition.

---

## 5. Reading Order vs. Chronology

**Recommended reading order:** Books 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 5 (Dark Tower model — meet him old, learn his beginning last).

**In-world chronology:** Book Five (the Sundering) → One → Two → Three → Four.

Either order is intended to work; the series is built so Book Five recontextualizes the first four on a reread, the same way Roland's Tower reframes the whole Dark Tower series.

## 6. Open Decisions for Later Drafting

- **Book Five's resolution fork** (Release / Cost / Ambiguous) — does not need to be locked until that book is drafted.
- **Possible Book Six (optional, post-capstone or interstitial):** The Emberlands' native fire-and-rebirth myth, under the Flame-Mother and Ash-Father — a natural companion volume to *The Drowned Kingdom*, following the same "elder native civilization, native magic origin" model.
- **Format per book:** chapter-by-chapter outline, ensemble cast list, and sample opening pages in Elowen's narrating voice — to be drafted one book at a time, starting with Book One.

## 7. Canon Firewall (Reminder for All Future Drafting)

This series draws **only** on:
- The native pantheon (Radiant Queen, Stonefather, Verdant Mother, Flame-Mother, Ash-Father)
- Native regional magic traditions (resonance, spoken-memory, tide-reading, fire-shaping, etc.)
- Native cultures and homelands (Nullwood, Tharundor, the coastal/sunken peoples, the Emberlands)

This series **excludes entirely**:
- Aeor, the Calamity, the dimensional rift, Exandria
- The Aeorian Echo, Eclipse Day, the cascade, the Heart of Winter, the White Maw
- Any current campaign-era NPC, location, or plot device

The goal throughout is deep-time legend that colors the world a player or DM already knows, without ever touching what they're currently playing through.
